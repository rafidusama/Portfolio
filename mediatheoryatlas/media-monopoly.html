1980s Political Economy

Media Monopoly and Concentration

Ben Bagdikian Journalist and dean, UC Berkeley Graduate School of Journalism
Historical context

Counting the owners, edition after edition

Ben Bagdikian spent much of his career as a working journalist before turning his attention to a question that seemed almost too simple: exactly how many companies actually owned America's newspapers, magazines, television and radio stations, book publishers, and film studios. In 1983, he published the first edition of The Media Monopoly, reporting that roughly fifty corporations controlled the majority of American media output across every major category.

Bagdikian did not stop there. He revised and republished the book across several subsequent editions over the following two decades, tracking that number as it shrank through wave after wave of mergers and acquisitions. By the late 1990s, the figure he reported had fallen to roughly ten corporations. By his final edition in 2004, retitled The New Media Monopoly, he put the number at around five major conglomerates controlling the majority of American media.

Bagdikian's central argument was not simply that fewer companies owned more outlets, though that alone concerned him. It was that this concentration mattered because it concentrated real political and cultural power in fewer hands, even as the total volume of available channels, magazines, and websites appeared to be expanding. Many seemingly distinct media brands, he argued, shared the same small set of ultimate corporate owners, whose broader business interests shaped editorial priorities in ways audiences rarely saw directly.

The book's central number, dropping from roughly fifty owners to roughly five across just two decades, became one of the most frequently cited statistics in media reform and media literacy discussions, a stark, easily grasped measure of a much larger structural shift.

What fed this theory
  • Bagdikian's original 1983 edition of The Media Monopoly
  • Successive revised editions tracking ownership consolidation through the 1990s and 2000s
  • Waves of media mergers and acquisitions across those same decades
  • Growing concern about the political implications of concentrated media power
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

Ownership concentration

The steady trend of media ownership consolidating into a shrinking number of very large conglomerates across successive decades of mergers and acquisitions.

Concept 02

The illusion of choice

Many distinct-seeming media brands and outlets can share the same small set of parent companies, creating an appearance of diversity that does not necessarily reflect genuine editorial or viewpoint diversity.

Concept 03

Cross-media and cross-industry ownership

Media conglomerates increasingly own non-media businesses as well, creating potential conflicts of interest whenever news coverage happens to touch on the parent company's other business interests.

Examples in practice

Where this pattern turned up

1983 to 2004

Bagdikian's own tracked numbers

The book's signature statistic, roughly fifty corporations controlling most American media in 1983, narrowing to roughly five by the final 2004 edition, is Bagdikian's own most cited and most reproduced finding.

Cross-ownership case studies

Coverage softened by conflicts of interest

Bagdikian and later media reform researchers documented specific instances where news coverage was altered or softened because it touched on a parent company's other business interests, direct illustrations of the cross-ownership concern.

Contemporary media reform research

Continuing the ownership mapping tradition

Media reform organizations continue Bagdikian's practice of mapping which conglomerates own which outlets, regularly finding a small number of companies still controlling the large majority of media most people actually consume.

Contemporary parallel

Concentrated digital platform ownership

Scholars extending Bagdikian's framework point to the concentrated ownership of digital distribution and advertising infrastructure itself, a small number of major technology companies, as a new, additional layer of concentration beyond the traditional media ownership he originally tracked.

In one sentence
The number of corporations controlling most American media narrowed dramatically across just two decades, concentrating enormous cultural and political power in very few hands.

The theory asks a reader to look past the sheer number of channels and brands on offer, and ask instead how many genuinely different owners actually stand behind them.

Where the theory ran into trouble

More channels, fewer owners, an unresolved tension

The pushback

Critics note that the sheer number of channels, websites, and platforms available to audiences has grown enormously even as ownership concentrated, complicating any simple story about shrinking choice, since audiences today can access a vastly wider range of content, including independent and international sources, than during the broadcast era Bagdikian first analyzed. Some media economists also argue that consolidation can fund genuine efficiencies, expensive investigative journalism or major technology investment, that fragmented, purely independent outlets often struggle to sustain on their own.

Debate also continues over whether ownership concentration necessarily translates into actual editorial bias or homogenization in practice, since concentrated owners sometimes still permit, or even encourage, genuinely diverse coverage across their properties for competitive or reputational reasons. Even so, Bagdikian's core number remains a widely cited entry point for asking a question that has only grown more relevant since his final edition: who actually owns what you are watching, reading, or scrolling through.

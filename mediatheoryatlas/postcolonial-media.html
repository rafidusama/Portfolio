Postcolonial Media Theory

Edward Said Literary and cultural critic, Columbia University
Historical context

A pattern of representation, centuries in the making

In 1978, the literary critic Edward Said published Orientalism, a sweeping study of how European, and later American, scholarship, literature, art, and journalism had represented the Middle East, North Africa, and Asia, a vast and varied region flattened for centuries under the single label of "the Orient." Said's argument was not that individual writers or scholars were simply biased or careless. It was that this body of representation, accumulated across generations of travel writing, colonial administration, academic study, and popular culture, formed a coherent, self-reinforcing system of knowledge.

Drawing on the philosopher Michel Foucault's ideas about discourse and the relationship between knowledge and power, Said argued this system did not simply describe "the Orient." It helped produce it, constructing a version of these societies as timeless, exotic, irrational, and fundamentally inferior to a supposedly rational, modern West. That construction was never politically neutral. It provided a ready-made intellectual justification for actual colonial and imperial control, a framework in which governing these societies could be presented as a civilizing mission rather than domination.

Said extended the argument directly into contemporary media in his 1981 book Covering Islam, analyzing how American and European news coverage of Islam and Muslim-majority societies relied on strikingly similar reductive, threat-focused tropes to the ones he had traced through nineteenth century scholarship. Later scholars pushed the framework further still, examining how film, television, and popular culture continued to draw on the same recurring stock images long after formal colonial rule had ended.

The broader field that grew out of Said's work, now generally called postcolonial media theory, asks a consistent question across very different media forms: whose interests does this particular representation of a region or people actually serve, and what older pattern of domination might it be quietly extending.

What fed this theory
  • Said's 1978 book, Orientalism
  • Michel Foucault's work on discourse and the relationship between knowledge and power
  • Said's 1981 follow-up, Covering Islam, extending the argument to contemporary news media
  • Decades of decolonization movements prompting a broader reassessment of colonial-era scholarship
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

Orientalism as discourse

A coherent, historically accumulated system of representation, not just isolated instances of bias, that constructs an entire region as fundamentally different from and inferior to "the West."

Concept 02

The self and other binary

Western identity is partly built by defining itself against an imagined, inferior "Oriental" other, a contrast that conveniently flatters the West's own self-image as rational and modern.

Concept 03

Knowledge as power

Following Foucault, Said argued that representation and expert knowledge about a region are never neutral, they are entangled with, and help justify, actual political and military domination over it.

Examples in practice

Where this pattern turned up

19th century Europe

Orientalist scholarship and travel writing

Said's own primary case studies, academic "Oriental studies" departments, colonial administrators' writings, and travel literature that together depicted the Middle East as timeless and exotic, conveniently in need of Western governance.

Covering Islam, 1981

Western news coverage of the Middle East

Said's own extension of the framework into contemporary journalism, analyzing how American and European press coverage of Islam relied on the same reductive, threat-focused tropes he had traced through earlier scholarship.

Hollywood film

Recurring stereotypes on screen

Researchers building directly on Said's framework, notably Jack Shaheen's extensive study of Arab representation in American film, documented decades of recurring stereotypes casting Middle Eastern characters as terrorists, exotic villains, or backward figures.

Contemporary parallel

Immigration and terrorism coverage

Contemporary media scholars have applied Said's framework to news coverage of immigration and international conflict, finding a similar civilizational framing that contrasts a rational, modern West against an implicitly threatening or backward other.

In one sentence
Centuries of Western scholarship and media have constructed "the East" as an exotic, backward counterpart to a supposedly rational West, a pattern that has helped justify real domination rather than simply describing these societies.

The theory asks a reader to notice when a story about a region relies on a familiar, ready-made script rather than the specific, complicated reality of the actual people and events involved.

Where the theory ran into trouble

A powerful lens, applied carefully or not

The pushback

Critics, including some scholars working within Middle Eastern studies itself, have argued Said's framework can flatten the West's own internal diversity of opinion, underplaying genuine dissent and critique that existed even within colonial-era Western scholarship. Some have pointed to a risk of a kind of reverse essentialism, where the theory itself treats "the West" as a single, monolithic actor in much the same way it accuses Orientalist scholarship of flattening "the East."

The concept has also been extended so widely since 1978, applied to many different regions, media forms, and historical periods, that some scholars argue it now requires careful, case-by-case application rather than serving as a single blanket explanation for any negative or simplified representation of a non-Western society. Used carelessly, the framework risks becoming exactly the kind of totalizing generalization it was originally built to criticize.

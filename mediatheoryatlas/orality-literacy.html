1982 Form & Technology

Orality and Literacy

Walter Ong Jesuit priest and scholar, published Orality and Literacy: The Technologizing of the Word in 1982
Historical context

A mind that has never seen a written word thinks differently

Walter Ong spent much of his career studying something most literate people cannot directly observe in themselves: what thought and speech are actually like in a culture that has never had writing at all, and what changes, irreversibly, once that culture acquires it.

Ong called sustained oral cultures primary oral cultures, to distinguish them from cultures like our own that remain saturated with spoken habits while never being untouched by literacy. In a primary oral culture, knowledge that is not spoken and repeated is simply lost, so thought organizes itself around whatever is memorable: rhythm, formula, proverb, repetition, and stock epithets, the rosy-fingered dawn of Homeric epic being Ong's favorite example.

Writing, Ong argued, does not simply record thought that already existed in the same form. It restructures consciousness itself, letting a thinker hold an idea still on a page, return to it, analyze it out of its narrative context, and build the kind of abstract, sequential, decontextualized argument oral cultures have no durable way to sustain.

This is the theoretical machinery underneath the opening argument of this atlas's own founding text, Plato's fear of writing. Socrates was, in Ong's terms, one of the last major thinkers formed by a still-oral world, watching, with real prescience, exactly the kind of cognitive restructuring literacy was about to perform on his culture.

What fed this theory
  • Ong's own background in classical rhetoric and the history of the trivium
  • Milman Parry and Albert Lord's fieldwork on living oral epic poets in the twentieth-century Balkans
  • Eric Havelock's earlier work connecting Homeric formula to the specific demands of oral memory
  • Marshall McLuhan, Ong's teacher, whose medium theory Ong extended and gave firmer historical grounding
  • Cross-cultural anthropological fieldwork among societies that had not yet adopted writing
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

Primary orality

A culture with no knowledge whatsoever of writing, where thought and expression are organized entirely around what a human memory, unaided, can retain and pass on.

Concept 02

Formulaic thought

Oral cultures rely on fixed epithets, proverbs, and rhythmic patterns, not because they lack imagination but because these are the only units of thought stable enough to survive being spoken and remembered rather than stored.

Concept 03

The restructuring of consciousness

Ong's central and most contested claim: literacy does not just add a new skill to an unchanged mind. It reorganizes how that mind is capable of thinking, favoring analysis, sequence, and abstraction over the oral world's formula and repetition.

Examples in practice

Where this idea was put to work

Ancient Greece

Homer's rosy-fingered dawn

Ong read the Iliad's stock epithets, not as poetic ornament but as load-bearing memory devices, formulas a reciting poet could reach for automatically to hold a line's meter and meaning together without a written text to check against.

Twentieth-century Balkans

Parry and Lord's living epic singers

Fieldwork among still-active oral epic performers showed them composing anew in performance using exactly the same formulaic techniques scholars had inferred from Homer, direct living evidence for Ong's account of oral composition.

Classical Athens

Plato's own written attack on writing

Ong's framework gives Plato's paradox its clearest explanation: Socrates represents a mind still largely shaped by oral habits, reacting with real alarm to a technology that was, even as he spoke, already restructuring the culture around him.

Contemporary reading

Secondary orality in broadcast and digital media

Ong coined secondary orality for the partial return of oral features, communal listening, formula, repetition, in radio, television, and later online video, a culture that has been through literacy and cannot fully return to a primary oral state.

Where the idea still shows up

A word for what podcasts and voice notes are actually doing

Ong's secondary orality is the standard term media scholars reach for when describing podcasts, voice messages, and livestream chat, media that sound oral but depend entirely on literate, technological infrastructure behind them.

His stronger claim, that literacy restructures cognition itself, remains genuinely contested, but the milder, more defensible version, that oral and literate cultures organize knowledge differently, still underwrites nearly every later theory in this atlas about how a medium's form shapes what it can carry.

Why this matters for media literacy
  • It supplies the historical and cognitive grounding underneath Medium Theory elsewhere in this atlas
  • Secondary orality is now standard vocabulary for analyzing podcasts, voice notes, and livestreamed speech
  • It gives the atlas's own opening chapter on Plato its full theoretical explanation
The mechanism, demonstrated

Orality and Literacy, in practice

Same mental task, two kinds of mind.

Pick a mental task, then switch between how Ong says an oral mind and a literate mind actually handle it.

In one sentence
Writing restructures consciousness.

Ong's own claim, stated as plainly as he ever stated anything. Not that writing records thought unchanged, but that it changes the shape thought is capable of taking in the first place.

Where the theory ran into trouble

The pushback

The pushback

Ong's strongest claim, that literacy produces a genuinely different kind of cognition, has drawn sustained criticism from scholars who argue it risks an evolutionary hierarchy, subtly ranking literate societies as more advanced or rational than oral ones.

Later fieldwork complicated the clean binary between oral and literate minds, finding many societies maintain sophisticated oral and written practices side by side without the sharp cognitive break Ong's framework implies.

Even critics generally keep Ong's more modest observation: a medium's basic form, the presence or absence of a fixed, reviewable text, measurably changes what kind of thought and argument a culture can sustain, a claim medium theory has never fully abandoned.

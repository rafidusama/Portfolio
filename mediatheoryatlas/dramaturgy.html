Dramaturgy

Erving Goffman Sociologist, published The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life in 1959
Historical context

A sociologist watches a waiter and sees an actor

Erving Goffman's 1959 book The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life opens with an image that has outlasted almost everything else in mid-century sociology: a waiter, composed and attentive while serving a table, who visibly relaxes, jokes, and drops the performance the moment he pushes through the kitchen door.

Goffman's central claim was that this is not a special case but the general condition of social life. People are always performing a version of themselves for whichever audience is currently watching, managing a front region where the performance happens and a back region where they can drop it, much the way actors move between stage and backstage.

He borrowed his entire vocabulary from the theater deliberately: performers, audiences, roles, scripts, props, costume, framing everyday interaction not as an occasional act but as the basic structure through which all social life gets organized and understood.

Crucially, Goffman did not treat this performance as dishonest. Presenting a managed version of yourself to a particular audience is, in his account, simply how social order gets produced and maintained; the alternative is not more authenticity but social chaos, since nobody could function if every private thought and impulse were visible to everyone at once.

What fed this theory
  • Goffman's own fieldwork observing service workers, asylum patients, and everyday social encounters
  • Symbolic interactionism, the sociological tradition treating meaning as produced through social interaction
  • Theatrical vocabulary, borrowed deliberately from the stage to describe ordinary social behavior
  • Postwar interest in the sociology of everyday life, distinct from earlier large-scale institutional sociology
  • Later applications of Goffman's framework to broadcast and, eventually, networked media
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

Front stage and back stage

The front region is where the performance happens for an audience; the back region is where the performer can relax, prepare, or contradict the front-stage version without being seen doing it.

Concept 02

Impression management

The active, ongoing work of controlling what an audience perceives, choosing which details to reveal, which to conceal, and how to frame the ones that are shown.

Concept 03

Audience segregation

People maintain different performances for different audiences and work hard to keep those audiences from mixing, since a front-stage performance collapsing in front of the wrong audience can be socially costly.

Examples in practice

Where this idea was put to work

Goffman's original case

The waiter and the kitchen door

Composed and deferential in the dining room, relaxed and unguarded the moment he steps into the kitchen. The literal front stage and back stage his theory is named after.

Broadcast era

Television personalities off camera

Media scholars applied Goffman almost immediately to broadcasting, noting how a news anchor's on-air composure functions as a front-stage performance distinct from behavior once the camera stops rolling.

Workplace life

Professional versus personal social media accounts

Maintaining a polished LinkedIn profile alongside a more casual personal account is a direct, modern instance of audience segregation, keeping different performances aimed at different audiences from colliding.

Contemporary reading

The curated social media feed

Later scholars, including Joshua Meyrowitz and, decades later, researchers studying platform self-presentation, have read the feed itself as an extended front stage, one where the back region keeps shrinking as more of life becomes potentially visible to some audience.

Where the idea still shows up

The theory that explains why the feed feels exhausting

Goffman's front stage and back stage distinction is the direct ancestor of contemporary discussions about being permanently on, the sense that a phone camera turns almost any moment into a potential front-stage performance with no reliable backstage left to retreat to.

Context collapse, the modern problem of a single social media post reaching parents, employers, and friends simultaneously, is essentially Goffman's audience segregation breaking down at platform scale, a single performance now facing every audience at once.

Why this matters for media literacy
  • It supplies the working vocabulary, front stage, back stage, impression management, still used in social media research
  • Joshua Meyrowitz's later work in No Sense of Place extended Goffman's framework directly to broadcast media
  • It anticipates, by six decades, the concept of context collapse now central to platform studies

Front stage

In one sentence
All the world is not, of course, a stage, but the crucial ways in which it isn't are not easy to specify.

Goffman's own careful hedge. He was not claiming life is literally theater, only that the theatrical metaphor explains more of ordinary social behavior than most people are comfortable admitting.

Where the theory ran into trouble

The pushback

The pushback

Critics have argued Goffman's framework risks reducing sincerity to just another kind of performance, leaving little conceptual room for authentic, unmanaged behavior, which some see as either too cynical or simply unfalsifiable.

Feminist and critical race scholars have pointed out that impression management is not evenly distributed; some groups face far higher stakes and far less freedom in managing how they are perceived than others, a dimension Goffman's original framework did not address directly.

Even so, Joshua Meyrowitz's extension of the theory to broadcast media, and later scholars' extension of it to networked platforms, kept dramaturgy central to media studies long after Goffman's own fieldwork subjects had left the stage.

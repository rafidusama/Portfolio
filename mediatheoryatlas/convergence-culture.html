Convergence Culture

Henry Jenkins Media scholar
Historical context

Convergence as culture, not just technology

By the mid-2000s, media convergence had become a familiar buzzword, usually describing a fairly narrow technical phenomenon: multiple media forms, phone, camera, internet, television, merging into single devices. Henry Jenkins, in his 2006 book Convergence Culture: Where Old and New Media Collide, argued this narrow, hardware-focused definition missed most of what was actually interesting about the shift.

Convergence, Jenkins argued, was equally a cultural and social phenomenon, describing how content increasingly flowed across multiple media platforms, how media industries cooperated across those platforms rather than staying siloed, and how audiences had become genuinely migratory, willing to go almost anywhere across a media landscape in search of the specific experiences they wanted from a story or franchise they cared about.

Drawing on his own decades of earlier research into media fandom, Jenkins introduced the concept of participatory culture, describing audiences who no longer simply consumed media passively but actively created, remixed, discussed, and extended it themselves, through fan fiction, wikis, remix videos, and organized fan communities. He paired this with transmedia storytelling, a single narrative universe deliberately developed across multiple platforms, film, games, comics, and web content, with each platform contributing a distinct, complementary piece of the larger story rather than simply repeating the same content in different packaging.

Jenkins also borrowed the concept of collective intelligence from the philosopher Pierre Lévy, describing how online fan communities could pool knowledge and expertise collectively, solving puzzles or piecing together a scattered transmedia narrative in ways no single audience member could manage alone.

What fed this theory
  • Jenkins's 2006 book, Convergence Culture: Where Old and New Media Collide
  • His decades of earlier research into media fandom and participatory audiences
  • Pierre Lévy's concept of collective intelligence
  • Early 2000s multiplatform entertainment franchises deliberately spreading content across media
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

Participatory culture

Audiences actively create, remix, and extend media content rather than simply consuming it passively, through fan fiction, wikis, remix videos, and organized communities.

Concept 02

Transmedia storytelling

A single narrative universe deliberately developed across multiple media platforms, with each platform contributing a distinct, complementary piece of the overall story rather than repeating the same content.

Concept 03

Collective intelligence

Online communities pooling knowledge and expertise collectively, piecing together scattered story details or solving problems in ways no single audience member could achieve alone.

Examples in practice

Where this pattern turned up

Jenkins's central case study

The Matrix franchise

Jenkins's frequently cited example of transmedia storytelling, with the core narrative deliberately spread across films, animated shorts, video games, and comics, each contributing pieces of the story the others did not fully cover.

Fan fiction and fan wikis

Participatory culture in practice

Jenkins's research on media fandom treats fan-authored stories and collaboratively built wikis as central examples of participatory culture, audiences actively extending and reworking stories rather than simply consuming them.

Early 2000s reality television

Multi-platform audience voting

Jenkins discusses early competition-format reality shows as an example of media companies deliberately courting audience participation across broadcast, phone voting, and online forums, as a coordinated convergence strategy.

Contemporary parallel

Modern transmedia franchises

Today's extensive interconnected film, television, and game franchises, alongside fan-driven wikis and remix culture on video platforms, are frequently cited as direct continuations of the participatory and transmedia patterns Jenkins identified.

In one sentence
A single story can spread across film, games, comics, and fan communities, with audiences actively remixing, extending, and collectively piecing it together, rather than simply watching one channel deliver it.

The theory treats audiences as genuine collaborators in a media franchise's meaning, not just its final destination, and treats the spread across platforms as a storytelling choice, not just a marketing tactic.

Where the theory ran into trouble

Whose labor, whose profit

The pushback

Critics note that participatory culture and fan engagement, while genuine and significant, largely occur within spaces and platforms owned and controlled by media corporations, who benefit commercially from audience labor and attention without necessarily sharing power or profit with the fan communities generating real value for the franchise, an echo of Smythe's audience commodity argument covered earlier in this atlas. Some scholars also argue transmedia storytelling in practice can prioritize marketing and franchise expansion over genuinely enriching each platform's contribution, sometimes producing convergence that feels more like content padding than meaningfully complementary storytelling.

The participatory ideal has also been critiqued for underestimating how unevenly distributed the time, skill, and resources needed to meaningfully participate in fan production actually are, meaning the most visible, celebrated fan contributions often come from a narrower, more privileged slice of the overall audience than the theory's inclusive framing might suggest.

1970s Effects Tradition

Agenda-Setting

Maxwell McCombs and Donald Shaw Communication researchers, University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill
Historical context

A small North Carolina town reopens the question of media power

By the late 1960s, the dominant view in communication research, shaped heavily by Klapper's limited effects paradigm, held that mass media had only a modest influence on what people believed. Maxwell McCombs and Donald Shaw suspected that view had been asking the wrong question all along. Rather than testing whether media could change opinions, they set out to test whether media could shape which issues people considered important in the first place.

During the 1968 United States presidential campaign, McCombs and Shaw surveyed undecided voters in Chapel Hill, North Carolina, asking them to name the key issues they thought the campaign should address. At the same time, the researchers systematically analyzed the amount of coverage nine major news sources, newspapers, magazines, and television newscasts, gave to each of those same issues.

The rank order of issues voters considered important lined up strikingly closely with the rank order of how much coverage those issues had actually received. Voters were not simply adopting whatever opinion the news pushed. They were adopting the news's sense of what mattered most, an idea the political scientist Bernard Cohen had anticipated in 1963 with a phrase McCombs and Shaw's study seemed to confirm empirically: the press may not be successful in telling people what to think, but it is stunningly successful in telling people what to think about.

A follow-up panel study in Charlotte, North Carolina in 1972 strengthened the case further, tracking coverage and public concern over time and finding that shifts in media emphasis tended to precede shifts in public concern, lending real weight to the claim that the influence ran from newsroom to public, and not simply the other way around.

What fed this theory
  • The 1968 Chapel Hill study of undecided voters during the presidential campaign
  • Bernard Cohen's 1963 observation about the press shaping what to think about
  • The 1972 Charlotte panel study, which added evidence about the direction of influence
  • Growing dissatisfaction with the limited effects paradigm's narrow focus on persuasion
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

The media agenda and the public agenda

The media agenda is the relative emphasis and prominence news coverage gives to different issues. The public agenda is how the audience ranks those same issues by importance. The theory claims the first strongly shapes the second.

Concept 02

Salience transfer

The mechanism at the theory's core: the relative weight media places on an issue transfers to the audience's sense of how important that issue is, without necessarily changing what the audience actually thinks about it.

Concept 03

Second-level agenda-setting

Later researchers extended the theory to argue media does not just make an issue salient, it also emphasizes particular attributes of that issue, shaping not only what audiences think about but which aspects of it come to mind first.

Examples in practice

Where this pattern turned up

North Carolina, 1968

The Chapel Hill study

The founding study behind the theory, comparing undecided voters' issue rankings against nine news sources' coverage volume, and finding the two rank orders lined up closely across categories like foreign policy, law and order, fiscal policy, public welfare, and civil rights.

North Carolina, 1972

The Charlotte panel study

A more rigorous follow-up tracking the same voters and the same news sources over time, adding real evidence about the direction of influence by showing shifts in coverage tending to come before shifts in public concern, not simply alongside them.

Crime coverage research

Local television news and public fear of crime

A frequently studied case in the agenda-setting literature: heavy local television emphasis on crime stories has repeatedly correlated with the public ranking crime as a top national concern, even in years when measured crime rates were actually falling.

Contemporary parallel

Cable news cycles and trending topics

Twenty four hour cable news and algorithmically generated trending topics function as a constantly updating, highly visible agenda, extending the same basic mechanism McCombs and Shaw described to a far faster and more fragmented news environment.

In one sentence
The more coverage an issue gets, the more important the public tends to think it is, whether or not their opinion about it ever changes.

The theory reframed the question of media power entirely. Instead of asking whether the news could change minds, it asked whether the news could decide what minds spend their time thinking about, a much harder claim to dismiss, and one the Chapel Hill data supported clearly.

Where the theory ran into trouble

Correlation is not quite the same as proof

The pushback

The original Chapel Hill study measured a correlation between two rankings at a single point in time, drawn from a small, non-random sample of undecided voters in one town. Critics immediately raised the obvious question: does news coverage really drive public concern, or does coverage simply track what an audience already cares about, or is the real relationship a two-way feedback loop between newsroom and public rather than a one-way arrow from one to the other.

The Charlotte panel study and later experimental work helped address that concern by tracking change over time rather than a single snapshot, and by the 1980s researchers including Shanto Iyengar and Donald Kinder pushed the question further still with controlled experiments, work that became its own theory later in this atlas. The line between second-level agenda-setting and framing theory has also stayed genuinely blurry, with researchers still debating exactly where emphasizing an issue's importance ends and shaping how that issue gets understood begins.

Framing Theory

Erving Goffman Sociologist, later applied to journalism by Robert Entman and others
Historical context

Deciding what is going on here

Erving Goffman spent his career studying the small, often invisible rules people use to make sense of everyday situations, and in his 1974 book Frame Analysis: An Essay on the Organization of Experience, he gave that process a name. A frame, in Goffman's sense, is the interpretive structure a person uses to answer a question they are usually not even aware of asking: what is going on here. The same physical actions, two people grappling on the ground, mean something entirely different depending on whether the frame in play is a sporting match, a street fight, or a rehearsal for a play.

Goffman's framework was originally about everyday social life, not journalism, but communication researchers quickly recognized its relevance to the news. A journalist deciding how to cover a story is, whether consciously or not, choosing a frame: which details to lead with, which sources to quote first, which words to use for the people involved, and which broader storyline the specific facts get slotted into.

The political scientist Robert Entman gave media framing its most widely cited definition in 1993, describing it as the act of selecting some aspects of a perceived reality and making them more salient in a way that promotes a particular problem definition, a particular explanation of its cause, a moral judgment about it, and a particular way of thinking about how to address it. Crucially, framing does not require leaving out facts or stating anything false. It works entirely through selection and emphasis of facts that are often all genuinely true.

Framing theory also draws real support from outside media research entirely. The psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky demonstrated in the 1980s that people offered the identical statistical outcome, framed once as a number of lives saved and once as a number of lives lost, made significantly different choices depending purely on which framing they heard, evidence that framing effects are not just a media studies theory but a documented feature of human judgment itself.

What fed this theory
  • Goffman's 1974 book, Frame Analysis: An Essay on the Organization of Experience
  • Robert Entman's influential 1993 definition of media framing
  • Kahneman and Tversky's experimental work on framing effects in decision making
  • Growing interest in how journalists' routine choices shape audience understanding without outright bias
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

The frame

An organizing structure that selects certain aspects of an issue and makes them more prominent, while quietly pushing other true, available aspects into the background.

Concept 02

Framing effects

Measurable shifts in how an audience interprets or judges an issue, caused purely by how the same underlying facts are organized and emphasized, with no new information added at all.

Concept 03

Frame building and frame setting

Frame building is the process by which journalists and institutions construct a frame in the first place. Frame setting is what happens next, as that frame goes on to shape how an audience actually understands the issue.

Examples in practice

Where this pattern turned up

Goffman's original example

A fight, a sport, or a rehearsal

The same physical grappling between two people reads entirely differently depending on the frame applied to it, evidence that meaning lives in the interpretive structure as much as in the raw observed action itself.

Entman's episodic versus thematic frame

One struggling family, or a broken system

Coverage of poverty that focuses on one family's individual hardship, versus coverage that discusses structural economic policy, can leave audiences with very different explanations for who or what is responsible for the same underlying social problem.

Kahneman and Tversky, 1981

Lives saved, or lives lost

Presented with mathematically identical outcomes of a hypothetical disease treatment, participants made significantly different choices depending on whether the outcome was framed in terms of lives saved or lives lost, a foundational finding behind framing effects generally.

Immigration coverage

"Illegal immigrant" versus "undocumented immigrant"

A frequently studied case of word choice alone shifting audience perception of the same underlying legal status, showing how a single term can carry an entire frame's worth of moral and political implication.

The event: a local factory lays off 200 workers this week
In one sentence
The same true facts can produce very different understandings, depending entirely on which details a frame chooses to emphasize.

The theory asks a reader to notice not just whether a story is accurate, but which of many accurate versions of the story they were given, and what that particular choice quietly suggests about who is responsible and what should happen next.

Where the theory ran into trouble

A useful concept stretched almost too far

The pushback

Framing effects are well documented in controlled experiments, but real audiences rarely encounter a frame on a blank slate. People bring their own existing beliefs and mental structures to a story, which can blunt, redirect, or even reverse the effect a given frame would otherwise have, an interaction later reception researchers explored in far more depth than framing studies alone typically capture.

The concept itself has also been criticized for spreading in so many directions, across psychology, sociology, and communication research, that some scholars argue framing has become difficult to define precisely enough to test rigorously. Its overlap with second-level agenda-setting, discussed earlier in this atlas, remains a genuine and still unresolved point of debate: researchers continue to disagree about exactly where emphasizing an issue's importance ends and shaping how that issue is understood begins.

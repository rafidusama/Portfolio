Social Learning Theory

Albert Bandura Psychologist, Stanford University
Historical context

The doll that changed how psychologists thought about watching

In 1961, Albert Bandura and his colleagues brought young children from a Stanford nursery school into a playroom, one at a time, and had each of them watch an adult in the room behave toward a large inflatable clown toy known as a Bobo doll. Some children watched the adult strike, kick, and throw the doll while shouting specific aggressive phrases. Others watched an adult play quietly nearby, ignoring the doll entirely.

Afterward, each child was left alone in a room with the same doll and a set of toys, and observed through a one-way mirror. Children who had watched the aggressive adult reproduced strikingly similar aggressive behavior toward the doll, often repeating the exact physical actions and phrases they had just seen, even though no one had told them to, and even though they received no reward for doing so. Children who had watched the calm adult showed almost none of that behavior.

This was a direct challenge to the era's dominant behaviorist psychology, which generally held that people needed to perform a behavior themselves and be rewarded or punished for it before they would learn it. Bandura's children learned an entire new behavior purely by watching, with no direct reinforcement of their own required at all.

Bandura extended the study in 1963, showing some children a live model, others a filmed version of the same adult, and others a costumed, cartoon-like character performing identical actions on film. Imitation rates stayed high across all three versions, a finding with obvious and immediate implications for television, which was by then a fixture in American homes.

What fed this theory
  • Bandura's 1961 Bobo doll experiment with children at a Stanford nursery school
  • The 1963 follow-up comparing live, filmed, and cartoon-character models
  • Growing unease about television's presence in the daily lives of young children
  • A broader shift in psychology away from strict behaviorism toward cognitive explanations
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

Observational learning

People, and especially children, can acquire an entirely new behavior simply by watching someone else perform it, with no need to try the behavior themselves first or receive any reward of their own.

Concept 02

Modeling

The demonstrated behavior of a model, live, filmed, or even a costumed character, that an observer may go on to imitate. How closely the observer identifies with the model affects how likely that imitation is.

Concept 03

Vicarious reinforcement

An observer learns not just the behavior itself but its likely consequences, by watching whether the model is rewarded or punished, adjusting their own likelihood of performing the behavior accordingly.

Examples in practice

Where this pattern turned up

Stanford, 1961

The original Bobo doll experiment

Children who watched a live adult behave aggressively toward the doll went on to reproduce specific aggressive actions and phrases far more than children who watched a calm adult, establishing observational learning as a genuine, measurable effect.

Stanford, 1963

Live, filmed, and cartoon conditions

Bandura's follow-up found that children imitated aggression seen on film, and even from a costumed cartoon-like character, nearly as readily as aggression they had seen performed live, directly implicating television and film as vectors for the same learning process.

Stanford, 1965

The vicarious reinforcement study

Children who saw the aggressive model rewarded imitated the behavior the most. Children who saw the model punished imitated it the least, at first, but when every child was later offered a reward to reproduce what they had seen, nearly all of them could do it, showing that learning and performance are not the same thing.

Later decades

Television and video game violence debates

Bandura's findings became a foundational reference point in later public and policy debates over media violence, cited repeatedly in government reports and content-rating discussions concerned with what children might learn from what they watched and played.

In one sentence
People can learn a whole new behavior simply by watching someone else perform it, no direct experience or reward required.

The theory reframed media, especially television, from a source of information or persuasion into something more direct: a steady supply of models whose actions viewers, particularly children, might absorb and reproduce, whether or not they were ever trying to.

Where the theory ran into trouble

A doll is not a person, and a lab is not a living room

The pushback

Critics pointed out that a Bobo doll is, by design, a toy meant to be hit, springing back upright and inviting exactly the kind of physical play the study measured, which raises real questions about how far the findings generalize to aggression against actual people. Decades of subsequent research into media violence and real-world aggression have found the relationship is real but considerably smaller, more contingent, and harder to isolate from a child's home environment, temperament, and existing behavior than the vivid, controlled lab results first suggested.

The laboratory setting itself drew scrutiny too. A child brought into an unfamiliar room, shown a brief demonstration, and observed immediately afterward is a long way from ordinary, everyday television viewing, embedded in family routines, commentary, and context that a one-time lab session cannot capture. Bandura himself later broadened the theory considerably, renaming it social cognitive theory in 1986 and adding concepts like self-efficacy and cognitive self-regulation that reached well beyond simple imitation, treating the observer as an active interpreter of what they watched rather than a straightforward copier of it.

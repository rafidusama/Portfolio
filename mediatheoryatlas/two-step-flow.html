1940s to 1960s Audience and Reception

Two-Step Flow

Paul Lazarsfeld and Elihu Katz Sociologists at Columbia University's Bureau of Applied Social Research
Historical context

The study that went looking for the needle and found something else

In 1940, Paul Lazarsfeld set out to measure the hypodermic effect directly. He and his team followed several hundred voters in Erie County, Ohio, through the Roosevelt versus Willkie presidential campaign, tracking exactly what they read, what they heard on the radio, and how their voting intentions moved over the following months.

The uniform, direct effect they expected to find was mostly absent. Most voters had already made up their minds before the campaign properly began, and mass media exposure barely moved the settled majority. The people whose opinions did shift were, again and again, responding to a conversation with someone they already trusted: a relative, a co-worker, a locally prominent neighbor, rather than to the broadcast or the newspaper directly.

Lazarsfeld and his co-authors published the finding in 1944 as The People's Choice, naming the pattern the two-step flow of communication: ideas move from media to a smaller set of attentive, socially connected people, and only then from those people to the rest of their circle. A decade later, Elihu Katz and Lazarsfeld returned to the idea with a dedicated study of opinion leadership in Decatur, Illinois, published in 1955 as Personal Influence, which mapped out who these go-betweens actually were across different areas of daily life, from choosing a movie to following public affairs.

The audience in this model is no longer a single, undifferentiated mass. It has structure: a smaller layer of attentive, well-connected people sits between the broadcast and everyone else, and that layer changes what the message means by the time it arrives.

What fed this theory
  • The 1940 Erie County panel study of the Roosevelt versus Willkie election
  • The People's Choice (1944), the book that introduced the two-step flow
  • Personal Influence (1955), Katz and Lazarsfeld's follow-up study in Decatur, Illinois
  • Growing postwar interest in social networks as a unit of research, not just individuals
  • The failure of the hypodermic model to show up under direct, careful measurement
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

Opinion leaders

A minority of people in any social circle pay closer attention to media than everyone around them, and are trusted enough that others check in with them before forming a view. They are not necessarily powerful or famous, just locally credible.

Concept 02

The two steps

Step one: media reaches the opinion leaders. Step two: opinion leaders, having already interpreted the message, pass a version of it on to the people who ask them or listen to them. Most people receive the second step, not the first.

Concept 03

Selective interpersonal influence

The opinion leader does not just relay the message, they filter it through their own judgment, their standing in the group, and what they think their circle needs to hear. The same broadcast can arrive very differently depending on who passes it on.

Examples in practice

Where this pattern turned up

Ohio, 1940

The Erie County election panel

The original study behind the theory. Repeated interviews across a single election season showed voters citing conversations with a trusted local figure far more often than they cited the radio or the newspaper directly as the reason their opinion moved.

Illinois, 1955

The Decatur opinion leadership study

Katz and Lazarsfeld found that opinion leadership was not one general trait. A woman who was the go-to voice on fashion in her circle was often not the same person others turned to for opinions on local politics or which film to see. Influence was local and topic-specific, not a single quality some people simply had.

Iowa, 1943

The hybrid seed corn study

A separate but closely related study by Bryce Ryan and Neal Gross tracked how Iowa farmers adopted a new type of hybrid seed corn. Most farmers did not decide based on the seed company's advertising. They waited to see what a handful of respected, early-adopting neighbors did first. The pattern fed directly into later diffusion research.

Public health campaigns

Trusted local figures as vaccine messengers

Health communication campaigns, from polio drives in the 1950s to more recent immunization efforts, have long relied on local doctors, teachers, and religious leaders to deliver a message that a national broadcast alone struggled to land. The strategy assumes exactly the two-step structure this theory described.

The message still starts at the tower. But this time it lands on one opinion leader first. Choose how the leader reacts, then fire, and watch how their reaction, not the raw broadcast, decides what the rest of the audience receives.

In one sentence
A message reaches most people only after passing through someone they already trust, so influence travels in two steps, not one.

The theory does not claim media has no effect. It claims the effect is filtered through a social layer first. Understanding an audience means understanding who its opinion leaders are, not just what the broadcast said.

Where the theory ran into trouble

Two steps turned out to be a simplification too

The pushback

Later researchers found that influence rarely moves in a clean two-step line. A message can pass through several layers of leaders and sub-leaders before it settles, and in some cases people skip the leader entirely and go straight to the original source. Scholars eventually proposed a more general "multi-step flow" to describe this, treating two steps as a special case rather than the whole picture.

Identifying an opinion leader in advance also proved harder in practice than the theory suggested. Influence turned out to be specific to a topic and a moment, so the same person could be an opinion leader on one subject and an ordinary audience member on another.

Networked digital media has complicated the picture further. When almost anyone can broadcast directly to a wide public, the clean line between "media" and "opinion leader" gets harder to draw. Some of that tension shows up later in this atlas, in theories built specifically around networks rather than a single broadcast source.

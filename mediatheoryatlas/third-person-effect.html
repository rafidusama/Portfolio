1980s Effects Tradition

Third-Person Effect

W. Phillips Davison Communication researcher
Historical context

A wartime leaflet, and a strange asymmetry in who it fooled

W. Phillips Davison introduced this idea in a 1983 article drawing on an observation he had made years earlier while researching wartime propaganda. Looking at archival material and interviews about enemy propaganda leaflets dropped during the Second World War, Davison noticed something curious. Officials and journalists who read the identical leaflets tended to believe those leaflets would sway the general public far more than the leaflets had swayed them personally, even though they had read the exact same words as everyone else.

Davison generalized this pattern into what he called the third-person effect. People consistently perceive persuasive or potentially harmful media messages, propaganda, advertising, biased news, violent content, as having a greater impact on other people than on themselves, even when they and those others were exposed to precisely the same message. The bias has two connected parts. The perceptual side is the belief itself, that "it affects them, not me." The behavioral side is what people often do as a result, supporting censorship, content warnings, or other restrictions aimed at protecting those presumed more vulnerable others, sometimes with little direct evidence that real harm to those others is actually occurring.

The theory struck a chord because it captured something that had shown up, unnamed, across a great deal of earlier communication research: a persistent gap between how people evaluate media's power over an abstract "public" and how they evaluate its power over themselves specifically.

What fed this theory
  • Davison's 1983 article, "The Third-Person Effect in Communication"
  • Archival research and interviews concerning Second World War propaganda leaflets
  • Decades of prior effects research that had never isolated this specific perceptual gap
  • Growing interest in the psychology of media perception, not just media effects themselves
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

The perceptual hypothesis

People consistently believe media messages influence other people more than they influence themselves, even when everyone involved was exposed to the identical content.

Concept 02

Self-other asymmetry

The gap between estimated effect on oneself and estimated effect on others tends to grow larger the more socially distant or unfamiliar that other group feels to the person making the judgment.

Concept 03

The behavioral hypothesis

This perceived gap often translates into real action, especially support for restricting or regulating media content, justified by protecting presumed vulnerable others rather than oneself.

Examples in practice

Where this pattern turned up

World War Two

Enemy propaganda leaflets

Davison's founding observation: officials who read the same leaflets as the general public consistently believed those leaflets would sway ordinary readers far more than the leaflets had swayed them personally.

Content regulation research

Explicit and violent media

Studies on violent or explicit content consistently find people rate themselves as far less affected than they believe "the average viewer" or "children" are affected, a pattern frequently cited to justify support for content warnings and restrictions.

Political campaign research

Negative advertising

Voters commonly believe attack ads sway other voters far more than themselves, even in controlled studies where the same voters can be shown to have measurably shifted their own views after exposure.

Contemporary parallel

Online misinformation

Recent research on social media finds a strikingly similar pattern: people widely believe other users are far more susceptible to online misinformation than they are themselves, a belief frequently cited in calls for stronger platform regulation.

In one sentence
People consistently believe media affects others more than themselves, even when everyone saw the identical message.

The theory turns attention away from what a message actually does and toward a separate, equally powerful question: how confidently people believe they are personally immune to something they assume is dangerous for everyone else.

Where the theory ran into trouble

A robust bias, with a real-world cost

The pushback

The third-person effect is one of the most consistently replicated findings in communication research, but critics note it can shade into a kind of quiet paternalism when it drives real policy, resting on the assumption that ordinary audiences are gullible while the person making that judgment is somehow personally immune. The self-reported nature of the "perceived effect" measure also makes it hard to verify against actual behavior, since believing yourself unaffected is not the same as actually being unaffected.

Later research also found the bias is not universal in direction. Some studies identified a reverse pattern, sometimes called a first-person effect, for content widely seen as socially desirable, such as public health messaging or educational programming, where people are more willing to claim it influenced them personally than to claim it influenced others. That finding suggests the underlying bias has less to do with media itself and more to do with whether admitting influence makes a person look foolish or makes them look responsible.

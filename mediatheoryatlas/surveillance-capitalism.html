Surveillance Capitalism

Shoshana Zuboff Social psychologist and business scholar, Harvard Business School
Historical context

A byproduct that became the business

Shoshana Zuboff traced what she considered a genuinely new economic logic back to a specific moment in the early 2000s, when engineers at Google realized something valuable in data they had originally treated as pure exhaust. Every search query left behind a trail, click patterns, time spent, follow-up searches, that had initially been seen as a mere byproduct of running a search engine, useful only for improving the search results themselves.

Google's engineers discovered this behavioral data exhaust could be repurposed into something far more valuable: increasingly precise predictions of what an individual user was likely to want, click, or buy next. Zuboff's 2019 book, The Age of Surveillance Capitalism: The Fight for a Human Future at the New Frontier of Power, argues this discovery marked the birth of a genuinely new economic model, one that spread rapidly across the technology industry once its profitability became clear.

Under surveillance capitalism, Zuboff argues, companies extract far more behavioral data than any service actually needs to function well, calling this surplus behavioral data. That surplus becomes the raw material for prediction products, sophisticated models of individual behavior sold in what Zuboff calls behavioral futures markets, to advertisers and other buyers interested not merely in predicting future behavior but in actively influencing it.

Zuboff explicitly connects her argument back to Dallas Smythe's much earlier audience commodity theory, covered previously in this atlas, while insisting her framework describes something qualitatively new rather than simply an intensified version of Smythe's broadcast-era insight, given the sheer scale of contemporary data extraction and its explicit goal of shaping behavior, not merely measuring an audience for advertisers.

What fed this theory
  • Zuboff's 2019 book, The Age of Surveillance Capitalism
  • Google's early 2000s discovery that behavioral data exhaust had independent commercial value
  • The rapid industry-wide adoption of data-driven, predictive advertising models
  • Dallas Smythe's earlier audience commodity theory, covered previously in this atlas
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

Behavioral surplus

Data extracted well beyond what is strictly necessary to provide a service, repurposed as raw material for building predictive products rather than simply improving the service itself.

Concept 02

Prediction products and behavioral futures markets

Sophisticated predictive models of individual behavior, built from behavioral surplus, sold in a new kind of marketplace to buyers interested in influencing, not just predicting, future behavior.

Concept 03

Instrumentarian power

Zuboff's term for a new form of power that works not through overt coercion but through subtle, pervasive shaping and nudging of behavior at scale, enabled by a massive data asymmetry between platforms and users.

Examples in practice

Where this pattern turned up

Early 2000s

Google's founding discovery

Zuboff's own central case study, tracing how engineers realized behavioral data exhaust from search queries could be repurposed into a hugely valuable predictive advertising product, the founding moment of surveillance capitalism in her account.

Political advertising

Behavioral micro-targeting

Discussed extensively by Zuboff and later researchers as a direct application of prediction products, raising particular concerns about democratic manipulation given the scale and precision of behavioral targeting now possible.

Smart home devices

Data extraction beyond the screen

Zuboff discusses the expansion of behavioral data extraction well beyond browsers and phones into physical home devices, extending surveillance capitalism's reach into previously unmonitored areas of daily life.

Contemporary parallel

Ongoing data privacy regulation

Debates over data privacy law, targeted advertising restrictions, and algorithmic transparency requirements are frequently framed using Zuboff's surveillance capitalism concept as the underlying economic logic policymakers are attempting to constrain.

In one sentence
A new economic logic extracts far more behavioral data than any service needs, turning that surplus into predictive products sold to shape, not just anticipate, what people do next.

The theory asks a reader to notice the gap between what a free service seems to give and how much it actually takes, and to ask what happens to a prediction once someone has bought it.

Where the theory ran into trouble

New name, old practice, or something genuinely different

The pushback

Some economists and technology scholars have questioned how genuinely novel surveillance capitalism really is as an economic category, versus being an intensified, digitally enabled extension of much older advertising and audience-measurement practices, directly echoing the debate already covered in this atlas over Dallas Smythe's earlier audience commodity theory. Critics have also questioned the actual predictive accuracy of many advertising-targeting systems in practice, suggesting the behavioral futures markets Zuboff describes may be less scientifically precise and less reliably powerful at shaping behavior than the most alarming accounts suggest.

Even with these ongoing scholarly disputes over its precise theoretical novelty, Zuboff's framework has proven highly influential in shaping contemporary regulatory debates and public awareness of data privacy issues, giving policymakers and the public a shared vocabulary for a set of practices that had previously been hard to name clearly.

1960s Form and Technology

Medium Theory

Marshall McLuhan Media theorist, University of Toronto
Historical context

The form was the argument, not the content inside it

Marshall McLuhan trained as a literary scholar, but by the early 1960s he had become convinced that media researchers were asking the wrong question. Everyone wanted to know what a message said and what effect that content had. McLuhan argued the far more important question was what kind of medium was carrying the message in the first place, because the medium itself, independent of any particular content, reorganizes how people perceive, think, and relate to one another.

He laid the historical groundwork in his 1962 book The Gutenberg Galaxy, arguing that the printing press did not simply spread new ideas faster, it restructured human consciousness itself. Print encouraged linear, sequential, individual reading, in sharp contrast to the communal, simultaneous, acoustic experience of oral cultures, and McLuhan traced a direct line from that shift to the rise of European individualism, standardized national languages, and modern nationalism.

In 1964, McLuhan sharpened the argument into its most famous form with Understanding Media: The Extensions of Man, where he coined the phrase "the medium is the message." His point was deliberately provocative: whether a broadcast carries calm news or urgent propaganda matters less, in McLuhan's view, than the sheer fact that it arrives instantly, everywhere, all at once, a structural property of the medium itself that reshapes society regardless of the specific words spoken.

McLuhan wrote at the height of television's dominance, and he treated the medium as the central case study of his era, distinguishing it sharply from radio, film, and print in ways that made his work required, if controversial, reading across advertising, design, and media industries almost as much as in universities.

What fed this theory
  • McLuhan's 1962 book, The Gutenberg Galaxy, on print's reshaping of consciousness
  • His 1964 book, Understanding Media, and its phrase "the medium is the message"
  • Television's rapid rise to cultural dominance through the 1950s and 1960s
  • McLuhan's own background in literary and rhetorical analysis, applied to technology
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

The medium is the message

The form a communication technology takes, not the particular content it happens to carry, is what genuinely reshapes how people think and how society organizes itself.

Concept 02

Hot and cool media

McLuhan classified media by sensory definition and the effort they demand: hot media deliver dense, high-definition information requiring little completion from the audience, cool media are lower-definition and pull the audience in to fill the gaps.

Concept 03

The global village

Electronic media compress time and distance, McLuhan argued, reviving something like the tightly interconnected, simultaneous awareness of a small tribal community, but stretched across the entire planet at once.

Examples in practice

Where this pattern turned up

Renaissance Europe

The printing press

McLuhan's argument in The Gutenberg Galaxy was never about any particular printed book. It was about what the technology of movable type itself did to European thought: standardizing language, encouraging linear reasoning, and laying groundwork for both modern science and modern nationalism.

1960s television

Television as a "cool" medium

McLuhan controversially classified television as cool, arguing its comparatively low-definition image demanded active perceptual participation from viewers, a claim many later media scholars disputed, but one that pushed researchers to take the medium's own form seriously as an object of study.

United States, 1960

The Kennedy-Nixon debate

Contemporary accounts and later scholarship have repeatedly pointed to reports that radio listeners and television viewers of the first Kennedy-Nixon presidential debate came away with different impressions of who had won, a case frequently cited as evidence that the medium itself, not just the candidates' words, shaped how the event was received.

Contemporary parallel

The social media feed as a medium

A character limit, an infinite scroll, and an algorithmic ranking system reshape political and social discourse simply through their own structure, regardless of what any individual post actually says, a direct extension of McLuhan's argument into a medium he never lived to see.

The theory asks media literacy to look past the headline entirely, at least for a moment, and ask a different question first: what does this particular technology, print, broadcast, or feed, do to any message simply by being the vessel that carries it.

Where the theory ran into trouble

Form matters, but it was never the only thing that mattered

The pushback

Critics, particularly researchers working in the political economy tradition, argued McLuhan's focus on form came at the expense of ownership, economics, and power. A medium's technical shape matters, they conceded, but who owns the printing press, who funds the broadcast tower, and who profits from the platform shape outcomes at least as much as whether the medium itself is print, radio, or television, a dimension McLuhan's framework had comparatively little to say about.

McLuhan's own hot and cool classification also drew sustained skepticism. His claim that television was a cool, low-definition medium requiring active viewer participation struck many later scholars as inconsistent, difficult to test, and arguably backward given how passively much television is actually consumed. His writing style, aphoristic, deliberately provocative, and often more interested in a striking phrase than a testable claim, made McLuhan enormously influential in popular and creative circles while leaving many social scientists uncertain how to verify his central arguments empirically.

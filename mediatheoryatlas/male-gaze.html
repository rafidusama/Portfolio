Male Gaze and Feminist Film Theory

Laura Mulvey Feminist film theorist and filmmaker
Historical context

Whose eyes is the camera really looking through

In 1975, the film theorist and filmmaker Laura Mulvey published a short, dense, deliberately provocative essay in the British journal Screen called "Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema." Drawing on psychoanalytic theory, particularly Freud's concept of scopophilia, pleasure in looking, and Lacanian ideas about identification, Mulvey argued that classical Hollywood cinema was not neutrally photographed. It was structured, at a fairly deep formal level, around a specifically masculine, heterosexual viewing position she called the male gaze.

Mulvey identified three intertwined looks that typically align in mainstream narrative film. The camera's look composes and frames a scene, often lingering on a woman's body in ways that fragment it into parts, a face, a leg, a silhouette, treated as visual spectacle rather than as an integrated, active person. The male characters' look within the story positions women as objects of desire that drive or reward the male protagonist's actions. And the spectator's look, Mulvey argued, is constructed by the film to align with that same masculine position, inviting audiences of any actual gender to identify with the active, desiring, controlling gaze of the male character rather than with the woman being looked at.

Mulvey coined a specific phrase for the resulting position women occupy in this structure: to-be-looked-at-ness. Rather than driving the plot forward as an active subject, the female figure in classical cinema is repeatedly staged as something to be visually displayed, her narrative function secondary to her function as spectacle.

The essay became one of the most cited and most argued-over texts in the history of film and media theory, spawning decades of follow-up work, including substantial reconsideration from Mulvey herself, who returned to the argument in a 1981 essay exploring how female spectators might actually engage with the same films.

What fed this theory
  • Mulvey's 1975 essay, "Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema," published in Screen
  • Psychoanalytic theory, particularly Freudian scopophilia and Lacanian identification
  • Close analysis of classical Hollywood conventions in staging and camera composition
  • The wider feminist theory movement of the 1970s applying new critical tools to culture
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

The male gaze

The camera, the male characters within a film, and the audience are all structured to share a single masculine, desiring viewpoint, regardless of who is actually sitting in the theater.

Concept 02

To-be-looked-at-ness

Mulvey's term for how classical cinema positions women primarily as objects of visual display, their narrative role secondary to their function as something for the camera and characters to look at.

Concept 03

Scopophilia and identification

The psychoanalytic concepts underlying the theory: pleasure in looking itself, and the way a film invites spectators to identify with the active, controlling gaze of its male protagonist.

Examples in practice

Where this pattern turned up

Classical Hollywood conventions

The staged entrance

Mulvey pointed to a recurring convention of introducing a female star through a moment of visual spectacle, narrative momentum pausing briefly for a look that exists primarily to display her, before the story resumes.

Alfred Hitchcock's films

The camera as an extension of the protagonist's gaze

Mulvey's essay draws heavily on Hitchcock's work, arguing that his camera repeatedly aligns with a male protagonist's own act of looking, structuring the audience's perspective to match his desire and, at times, his suspicion.

Advertising and music video analysis

The gaze beyond narrative film

Later scholars extended Mulvey's framework well past cinema, finding the same fragmenting camera conventions, close-ups on isolated body parts, poses staged purely for display, recurring across advertising imagery and music videos.

Contemporary parallel

The "female gaze" and its critics

Contemporary film criticism and filmmaking have explored what a female gaze might look like in contrast, while other scholars have extended and complicated Mulvey's original binary framework through the lens of race and queer theory.

In one sentence
Classical narrative cinema is built, at the level of camera, character, and audience, around a masculine viewing position that turns women into spectacle rather than active subjects.

The theory asks a viewer to notice not just what a scene shows, but whose eyes the camera seems to be borrowing, and what gets lost when one figure exists mainly to be looked at rather than to act.

Where the theory ran into trouble

Not every viewer sits in the same seat

The pushback

Critics, including Mulvey herself in later work, pointed out that the original essay assumed a single, universal heterosexual male spectator position, leaving little room for how women, queer viewers, and viewers of color actually experience the same films, whose engagement does not necessarily match the theorized gaze at all. Mulvey's own 1981 follow-up essay directly reconsidered the question of female spectatorship, an acknowledgment that the original binary was doing less explanatory work than it first appeared to.

The psychoanalytic framework underlying the theory has also drawn sustained criticism as difficult to test empirically, resting on Freudian and Lacanian assumptions some scholars consider culturally narrow or dated. More recent scholarship, including work on a possible female gaze and postcolonial and queer readings of cinematic looking, has substantially expanded and complicated Mulvey's original framework rather than simply replacing it.

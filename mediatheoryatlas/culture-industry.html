1940s Political Economy

Culture Industry

Theodor Adorno & Max Horkheimer Frankfurt School philosophers, wrote in exile in the United States during the Second World War
Historical context

Two refugees look at Hollywood and see a factory

Theodor Adorno and Max Horkheimer fled Nazi Germany and washed up, of all places, in Los Angeles, where they watched American popular culture, radio serials, Hollywood films, popular music, with the eyes of people who had just watched a modern industrial society turn a genocidal regime into mass entertainment of its own kind.

In Dialectic of Enlightenment, written during the war and published in 1944, they coined the term culture industry deliberately, in place of the more common phrase mass culture, because they wanted to insist the culture in question was not something rising spontaneously from the people but something manufactured, on an assembly line, to a formula, the same way a factory manufactures cars.

Their claim was not simply that popular culture was bad art. It was that the culture industry produces standardized products dressed up as individual expression, trains audiences to expect and want exactly that standardization, and in doing so forecloses the kind of critical, independent thought a healthy public would need to resist the political currents Adorno and Horkheimer had just watched sweep through Germany.

Entertainment, in their account, is not an escape from work but an extension of it: a way of using leisure time to recover just enough energy to return to the factory or the office, structured by the same logic of efficiency, repetition, and predictability that governs the working day itself.

What fed this theory
  • Direct, personal experience of Nazi propaganda and the collapse of Weimar German culture
  • Exile in Los Angeles, at the heart of the American commercial entertainment industry
  • The standardization and interchangeability of Hollywood genre films and radio formats in the 1930s and 1940s
  • Karl Marx's account of commodity production and the assembly line
  • A deep skepticism, shared across the Frankfurt School, toward both fascism and unregulated market capitalism
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

Standardization

Products of the culture industry look individual but are built from a small set of interchangeable formulas. A popular song, a detective film, and a radio drama each promise novelty while delivering a structure the audience has already consumed a hundred times.

Concept 02

Pseudo-individualization

The industry markets minor variations, a different star, a new setting, a fresh hook, as if they were meaningful differences, so audiences feel they are choosing and being told something new when they are really consuming the same formula again.

Concept 03

The eclipse of critical thought

By training audiences to expect easy resolution and immediate gratification, the culture industry weakens the appetite and capacity for the sustained, difficult thinking a critical public needs, leaving audiences more receptive to propaganda and less able to imagine alternatives to existing arrangements.

Examples in practice

Where this idea was put to work

1930s and 1940s

The Hollywood studio system

Vertically integrated studios produced genre films to formula, on tight schedules, with recurring stars and interchangeable plots. Adorno and Horkheimer read this literally as a factory system applied to culture, complete with division of labor and quality control.

Interwar Germany

Radio and the rise of fascism

Adorno had watched, before fleeing, how centralized broadcasting could be turned into an instrument of a single political will, reinforcing his suspicion that mass media technologies were never neutral tools waiting to be used well or badly.

Postwar United States

The popular music industry

Adorno's separate writing on popular music argued that its rigid verse-chorus structures and formulaic hooks trained listeners in passive, superficial listening, in sharp contrast to the demanding, structurally serious listening classical music required.

Ongoing

Genre franchises and algorithmic content

Later critics have applied the same argument to sequel-driven film franchises and to platform content optimized by engagement algorithms, arguing both represent the culture industry's logic pursued by newer, more efficient means.

Where the idea still shows up

A theory streaming platforms make easy to test

Critics of franchise filmmaking, algorithmically generated playlists, and reality television formats built for maximum interchangeability across markets are, whether they know it or not, restating Adorno and Horkheimer almost word for word.

The theory's harder edge, that this is not just aesthetically disappointing but politically dangerous because it dulls the capacity for critical thought, is more contested, and later cultural studies scholars pushed back hard on the idea that audiences are simply passive recipients of standardized meaning.

Why this matters for media literacy
  • It supplies the vocabulary, standardization, formula, pseudo-individualization, still used to critique streaming-platform content strategy
  • It is the direct ancestor of later political-economy critiques of media ownership and concentration
  • Stuart Hall's encoding and decoding model developed partly as a rebuttal to the passive audience this theory assumes

In one sentence
Something is provided for all so that none may escape.

Adorno and Horkheimer's own line. The culture industry does not need to please everyone with the same product; it only needs a large enough menu of standardized options that everyone finds something familiar enough to consume.

Where the theory ran into trouble

The pushback

The pushback

The most consistent criticism is elitism: Adorno in particular is frequently accused of simply disliking popular taste and dressing that dislike up as social theory, while treating difficult, high modernist art as automatically more emancipatory.

British cultural studies, especially Stuart Hall's encoding and decoding model, pushed back hard on the passive audience the culture industry thesis assumes, arguing real audiences negotiate, resist, and rework mass-produced meanings rather than simply absorbing them.

Even so, the underlying claim that media ownership structures shape what gets made long before any individual audience member has a chance to respond to it became a founding premise of the political economy tradition this atlas covers under Media Monopoly and the Propaganda Model.

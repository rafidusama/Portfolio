1980s Political Economy

Audience Commodity

Dallas Smythe Political economist
Historical context

The product was never really the program

Dallas Smythe spent decades studying the economics of communication, and by the late 1970s he had grown frustrated with what he saw as a genuine blind spot running through most media criticism, including a great deal of Marxist media analysis. Scholars had spent enormous energy analyzing the ideological content of commercial media, what messages and values it carried, while largely overlooking a much more basic economic question: what is actually being bought and sold here in the first place.

In his 1977 essay, "Communications: Blindspot of Western Marxism," Smythe proposed a genuinely uncomfortable answer. Commercial, ad-supported media does not primarily sell programming to audiences. It sells audiences to advertisers. The actual commodity changing hands in the core economic transaction of commercial broadcasting is not the show itself, but the packaged, measured attention of the people watching it, delivered to advertisers who are the media company's real paying customer.

Smythe pushed the argument further still. If audiences are the commodity being sold, then the act of watching, forming brand awareness, developing consumption habits, functions as a kind of labor, work that generates real economic value for both the media company and the advertiser, even though the audience performs it for free and rarely thinks of themselves as working at all while they do it.

The essay reframed the entire economic structure of commercial media. Content, in this view, exists primarily as the mechanism for assembling an audience efficiently enough to sell, the way a store might use an appealing display to draw in shoppers, rather than as the actual product the business is built around.

What fed this theory
  • Smythe's 1977 essay, "Communications: Blindspot of Western Marxism"
  • His broader career studying the economics and regulation of broadcast communication
  • Frustration with ideology-focused media criticism that overlooked the underlying business model
  • The rise of ratings systems as the literal marketplace mechanism for selling audiences
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

The audience commodity

What media companies actually sell to advertisers is not content, it is audience attention itself, packaged and measured as a commodity with a real, quantifiable market price.

Concept 02

Unpaid audience labor

Watching media, forming brand awareness, and developing consumption habits functions as a form of genuinely valuable labor that audiences perform for free, without ever perceiving themselves as working.

Concept 03

The blindspot of ideology-only analysis

Smythe's critique of media scholars who focused heavily on content and ideology while overlooking the basic economic transaction underlying commercial media's entire business model.

Examples in practice

Where this pattern turned up

The Nielsen ratings system

Audiences, literally priced and sold

The actual marketplace mechanism Smythe pointed to: audience size and demographics, measured through ratings, function as the real currency media companies sell to advertisers, matching his argument almost exactly.

Commercial broadcast television

Smythe's central example

The entire economic structure of free-to-air, ad-supported television, Smythe's most direct and thoroughly analyzed case of the audience commodity relationship in action.

Contemporary parallel

Social media engagement metrics

Modern platforms' business models, selling user attention and engagement data to advertisers, are frequently cited by contemporary scholars as a direct, intensified extension of Smythe's audience commodity concept.

Targeted advertising and data collection

Commodification taken further still

The practice of tracking user behavior to sell precisely targeted audience segments represents audience commodification pushed considerably further than the broadcast era Smythe was originally analyzing.

The mechanism, demonstrated

Follow the money, not the program

Two ways to picture the same transaction

Toggle between the common assumption about how commercial television works, and the transaction Smythe argued was actually happening underneath it.

In one sentence
In commercial media, the real product being sold is not the content, it is the audience's own attention, packaged and sold to advertisers.

The theory reframes a familiar, comfortable arrangement, free entertainment in exchange for a few commercial breaks, as an economic transaction in which the viewer is not really the customer at all, but the good being sold.

Where the theory ran into trouble

Is watching really work

The pushback

Some scholars questioned whether "watching" genuinely constitutes labor in the same meaningful sense as paid work, given that audiences engage voluntarily and often derive real enjoyment and value from the content itself, complicating a straightforward exploitation framing. Calling an evening spent enjoying a favorite show "unpaid labor" struck some critics as stretching the concept of work past its useful limits.

Other researchers in the political economy tradition embraced and extended Smythe's insight considerably further, particularly in more recent work on digital labor and data extraction, arguing his core argument has become even more directly applicable in today's data-driven advertising economy than it was in the broadcast era he was originally describing. Debate continues over how much weight to place on the specific language of labor and exploitation, versus simply treating audience attention as a valuable resource being monetized without necessarily framing every viewer as an unwitting worker.

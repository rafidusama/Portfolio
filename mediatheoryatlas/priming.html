Priming Theory

Shanto Iyengar and Donald Kinder Political scientists, Yale University and later Stanford and the University of Michigan
Historical context

Editing the news itself to find out what it does

Agenda-setting research had established, fairly convincingly by the early 1980s, that heavier news coverage of an issue made audiences consider that issue more important. Shanto Iyengar and Donald Kinder wanted to push the question one step further: did that same heavier coverage also change how people judged their political leaders, specifically by making them weigh that particular issue more heavily when forming an overall opinion.

Rather than relying on surveys of naturally occurring news exposure, Iyengar and Kinder ran a series of tightly controlled laboratory experiments through the early and mid-1980s. Participants watched edited television newscasts that the researchers had deliberately loaded with more or less coverage of specific issues, defense spending, inflation, civil rights, and others, while holding everything else about the broadcast constant.

The results, published in their 1987 book News That Matters: Television and American Opinion, showed something beyond simple agenda-setting. Participants exposed to heavier coverage of a given issue not only rated that issue as more important, they also weighted it more heavily when evaluating the sitting president's overall job performance, even when their actual opinion about the issue itself had not shifted at all.

This distinction mattered. Agenda-setting explained what issues came to mind. Priming explained which of those issues people actually reached for as the yardstick for judging a leader, a mechanism the researchers argued worked largely through cognitive accessibility: recently and repeatedly primed information sits closer to the surface of memory, and is more likely to get used as a mental shortcut when someone is asked to form a quick, overall judgment.

What fed this theory
  • The established findings of agenda-setting research through the 1970s
  • Iyengar and Kinder's controlled experiments with edited television newscasts
  • Their 1987 book, News That Matters: Television and American Opinion
  • Cognitive psychology research on memory accessibility and mental shortcuts
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

Priming

Exposure to a particular issue in the news increases the weight that issue carries in later, related judgments, especially evaluations of political leaders and institutions.

Concept 02

Accessibility

Recently and repeatedly primed information becomes easier to recall, making it more likely to be used as a mental shortcut the next time someone needs to form a quick overall judgment.

Concept 03

Applicability

A primed issue gets used as an evaluative criterion specifically when it feels relevant to the judgment at hand, such as holding a president responsible for the economy but not for an unrelated personal matter.

Examples in practice

Where this pattern turned up

Yale, early 1980s

The core newscast experiments

Iyengar and Kinder's original lab studies, varying how much coverage participants saw on issues like defense spending and inflation, then measuring how heavily those same issues factored into participants' overall approval ratings for the president.

1980s foreign policy coverage

Priming presidential approval on defense

Studies building on Iyengar and Kinder's research program found that heavy coverage of defense and foreign policy crises shifted which issues voters used to judge Ronald Reagan, separate from whether their opinion of any single policy had actually changed.

Political campaign strategy

Choosing which issues to emphasize before an election

Campaigns and communications teams that deliberately emphasize certain issues, the economy, national security, in the weeks before a vote or approval period are, consciously or not, applying the same priming logic Iyengar and Kinder documented in the lab.

Contemporary parallel

The rapid rotation of the cable news cycle

Twenty four hour news cycles that shift rapidly between an economic story, a scandal, and a foreign crisis create a faster, more compressed version of the same priming mechanism, changing which criteria audiences reach for to judge leaders almost week to week.

In one sentence
Heavy coverage of an issue does not just make it feel important, it makes people judge leaders specifically by how well they handle it.

The theory adds a second layer onto agenda-setting: it is not just about what audiences think about, but about which of those things they actually use as the measuring stick when evaluating the people in charge.

Where the theory ran into trouble

A clean lab result meets a messy real world

The pushback

Like agenda-setting before it, priming was demonstrated using carefully edited newscasts in a controlled laboratory setting, which raises the standard question of how cleanly the effect carries over into an ordinary media environment full of competing frames, conversations with other people, and each viewer's own strong prior political identity, all of which a brief lab exposure cannot fully replicate.

Later research also found priming effects can fade fairly quickly once heavy coverage of an issue stops, and that strongly partisan viewers may be primed quite differently, or resist priming altogether, depending on whether the primed issue reflects well or poorly on the party they already support. That complicates the relatively uniform effect the original lab experiments found, suggesting real-world priming is considerably more conditional than the tightly controlled studies first suggested.

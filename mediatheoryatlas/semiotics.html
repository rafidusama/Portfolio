Semiotics in Media

Roland Barthes French literary theorist and semiotician
Historical context

Reading a soap advertisement like a sentence

Roland Barthes built his approach on a set of ideas developed decades earlier by the linguist Ferdinand de Saussure, who proposed that a sign is made of two parts: a signifier, the physical form a sign takes, a word, a sound, an image, and a signified, the concept it brings to mind. Put together, a signifier and a signified make meaning. Barthes's contribution was to take this tool, built originally to study language, and turn it loose on the everyday texture of postwar French culture.

Through the 1950s, Barthes wrote a regular newspaper column examining ordinary cultural artifacts, a wrestling match, a bar of soap, a plate of steak and fries, a magazine cover, treating each one as a sign system worth close reading. He collected these columns in 1957 as Mythologies, a book that argued popular culture was full of what he called myth: a second layer of meaning, riding on top of a literal description, that made a specific cultural or political viewpoint feel like plain, natural common sense.

His most famous single example, from the book's closing theoretical essay, involved a photograph on the cover of the magazine Paris Match: a young Black soldier in French uniform, saluting, eyes lifted, presumably toward a French flag. Read literally, the image simply shows a soldier saluting. Read as myth, Barthes argued, it quietly communicates something much larger: that France is a great empire, and that all of its sons, regardless of race, serve it loyally, an image that naturalizes French colonial power by presenting it as a simple, uncontroversial fact rather than a specific and contestable political claim.

Barthes extended the method further in a later essay, "Rhetoric of the Image" from 1964, which analyzed a print advertisement for Panzani pasta sauce in close detail, showing how color, composition, and the objects chosen for the photograph combined to communicate ideas like freshness and Italian culinary authenticity well beyond anything the ad's words actually said.

What fed this theory
  • Ferdinand de Saussure's foundational work on the sign, the signifier, and the signified
  • Barthes's newspaper columns on French popular culture, collected in 1957 as Mythologies
  • Postwar France's rapidly expanding consumer culture and advertising industry
  • Barthes's 1964 essay "Rhetoric of the Image," which extended the method to advertising
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

Signifier and signified

A sign is built from two parts working together: the physical form it takes, and the concept it calls to mind. Neither means anything on its own. Meaning happens in the pairing.

Concept 02

Denotation and connotation

Denotation is the literal, descriptive meaning of an image or text. Connotation is the cultural and emotional associations layered on top of that literal meaning, often doing far more communicative work than the literal description itself.

Concept 03

Myth

The process by which a connotation stops looking like one particular cultural viewpoint among many, and starts looking like plain, obvious, natural common sense, quietly carrying an ideology while appearing to carry nothing at all.

Examples in practice

Where this method turned up

Paris Match, 1955

The saluting soldier

Barthes's central example of myth in action. A literal photograph of a soldier saluting becomes, at the level of myth, a naturalized statement about the benevolence and loyalty owed to French empire, with the specific political claim disguised as simple observation.

"Rhetoric of the Image," 1964

The Panzani advertisement

Barthes broke down a print ad for pasta sauce showing a net bag spilling fresh vegetables, finding that its colors, framing, and composition connoted freshness and Italian culinary tradition well beyond what the product photograph literally depicted.

Mythologies, 1957

Professional wrestling

In his essay "The World of Wrestling," Barthes argued the sport was never really about athletic competition. It functioned as pure spectacle, a performance of exaggerated signs, suffering, treachery, justice, meant to be read symbolically rather than watched as a real contest.

Contemporary parallel

Brand logos and color palettes

A swoosh, a specific shade of red, a particular typeface can instantly connote speed, luxury, or rebellion without a single word of text, a routine, everyday demonstration of exactly the layered sign systems Barthes was describing more than sixty years ago.

In one sentence
Every image says something literal and something cultural at the same time, and the cultural part often works hardest by pretending to be nothing at all.

Barthes's method gives media literacy a concrete habit: pause on the literal description of an image first, then ask what feelings or ideas are riding along with it, and finally ask whose viewpoint that feeling is quietly presenting as simple common sense.

Where the theory ran into trouble

A brilliant reading is still one reading

The pushback

Critics pointed out that semiotic analysis, for all its insight, is fundamentally a method performed by a single trained critic sitting with a single text. It offers no built-in way to check whether ordinary audiences actually perceive the connotations a critic identifies, or arrive at very different readings of their own. Barthes's soldier saluting a flag might communicate naturalized empire to one reader and something else entirely to another, and the method alone cannot settle which reading is more common, or why.

That gap helped motivate the next major shift in media research, away from analyzing texts in isolation and toward studying how real audiences actually interpret what they see. Stuart Hall's encoding and decoding model, and the reception studies that followed it, can be read directly as a response to this limitation, asking not just what a sophisticated reading can find inside an image, but what actual audiences, differently positioned by class, background, and belief, make of it in practice.

1970s Political Economy

Cultural Imperialism

Herbert Schiller Media scholar, University of California, San Diego
Historical context

A one-way current, disguised as an open exchange

Herbert Schiller spent his career studying who actually controlled the global flow of media, entertainment, and information, and by the late 1960s he had reached a stark conclusion. In his 1969 book Mass Communications and American Empire, and more fully in his 1976 follow-up Communication and Cultural Domination, Schiller argued that postwar global media flow ran overwhelmingly in one direction, from a small number of wealthy, powerful nations, above all the United States, outward to the rest of the world.

That imbalance was not, in Schiller's view, a neutral accident of who happened to produce the most popular content. Hollywood film, American television programming, Western news wire services, and American-style advertising did not just entertain or inform audiences abroad, they carried a whole set of values, consumerist assumptions, and political and economic interests along with them, often at the direct expense of local media production and cultural traditions in developing nations.

Schiller's argument landed as a direct, pointed challenge to development and modernization theory, covered earlier in this atlas. Where Lerner and Schramm had framed growing media exposure as a benign, even beneficial engine of national development, Schiller argued the same flow of media, examined through a political-economy lens rather than a development lens, looked much more like a new, subtler form of imperial domination, operating through culture and communication rather than direct colonial administration, but serving remarkably similar interests.

He coined the term cultural imperialism to describe this pattern, treating the postwar global media system as a continuation of older imperial relationships by other means, with American corporations and the American government replacing the colonial powers of an earlier era as the primary beneficiaries.

What fed this theory
  • Schiller's 1969 book, Mass Communications and American Empire
  • His 1976 follow-up, Communication and Cultural Domination
  • Postwar American dominance of global film, television, and news wire distribution
  • Direct critique of the optimistic assumptions behind development and modernization theory
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

One-way media flow

The overwhelming direction of global media, entertainment, and news content moving from wealthy, media-exporting nations outward to the rest of the world, rather than any genuine two-way exchange.

Concept 02

Cultural domination

That one-way flow spreads the exporting nation's values, consumerist ideology, and political-economic interests, often displacing or marginalizing local culture and local media production in the process.

Concept 03

A successor to colonialism

Schiller's core claim: cultural and media domination can extend and serve the same underlying power interests that direct colonial political control once did, just through considerably subtler means.

Examples in practice

Where this pattern turned up

Global box office and television

Hollywood's export dominance

Schiller's central, recurring example: American entertainment content dominating screens worldwide through the postwar decades, while the reverse flow of non-American content reaching American audiences remained comparatively minimal.

Global wire services

Western news agencies as gatekeepers

Schiller and other scholars pointed to the outsized role of a handful of Western news agencies in supplying the majority of the world's international news, shaping how much of the globe came to understand distant events, and each other, primarily through a Western lens.

Global advertising

Exporting consumer culture

The spread of American-style advertising techniques and consumerist values through global media was studied as its own distinct mechanism of cultural domination, separate from entertainment content but working toward similar ends.

Contemporary parallel

Streaming and social platform dominance

Contemporary scholars frequently point to the current global market dominance of American streaming platforms and social media companies as a direct continuation of the same one-way cultural flow Schiller described decades earlier, adapted to an on-demand digital environment.

In one sentence
Global media has flowed overwhelmingly outward from a handful of wealthy exporting nations, carrying values and interests along with the entertainment.

The theory asks a simple, pointed question of any media system: who is exporting culture to whom, and whose interests does that direction of flow ultimately serve.

Where the theory ran into trouble

The flow got a lot less one-way

The pushback

The one-way flow model, dominant through the 1970s and 1980s, ran into serious trouble as global media production diversified considerably in the decades that followed. Latin American telenovelas, Indian cinema, Japanese anime, and Korean popular culture all achieved substantial audiences well beyond their home markets, complicating a framework built almost entirely around a single, dominant American export flow.

Reception researchers, including David Morley, covered earlier in this atlas, raised a separate and equally serious objection: Schiller's framework concentrated heavily on the political economy of who produced and exported media, but had comparatively little to say about how local audiences actually received, reinterpreted, or resisted imported content, rather than passively absorbing whatever values it supposedly carried. Later theorists proposed concepts like cultural hybridity and glocalization to describe a messier, more genuinely two-way reality that a purely one-way imperialism model struggled to capture.

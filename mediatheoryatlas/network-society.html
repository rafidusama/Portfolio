Network Society Theory

Manuel Castells Sociologist
Historical context

A new structure organized around connection itself

By the mid-1990s, the sociologist Manuel Castells had spent years watching digital information and communication networks reshape economic and social life in ways that older sociological categories, nations, firms, fixed geographic communities, struggled to fully capture. In 1996, he published The Rise of the Network Society, the first volume of his ambitious trilogy The Information Age, arguing that a genuinely new kind of social structure had emerged, one organized fundamentally around networks rather than the more centralized, hierarchical institutions that had defined earlier industrial society.

Castells introduced a specific pair of concepts to capture the tension he saw at the heart of this new structure. The space of flows describes how power, capital, information, and cultural symbols increasingly circulate through global electronic networks, moving instantaneously across vast distances in ways that operate somewhat independently of any single physical location. The space of places, by contrast, describes the older, more familiar reality that most people's actual, lived, everyday experience remains rooted in specific physical locations, a neighborhood, a workplace, a home.

The friction between these two spaces became one of Castells's central claims. A relatively small, highly connected elite manages and benefits from the space of flows, global finance, multinational corporate networks, major media and technology hubs, while the majority of people and places remain comparatively fixed in the space of places, increasingly disconnected from the flows of capital and information that shape their circumstances from a distance.

Being connected, or excluded, from key global networks became, in Castells's framework, one of the central new axes of power and inequality in this emerging social structure, a claim that later researchers would extend directly into digital divide research, already explored earlier in this atlas.

What fed this theory
  • Castells's 1996 book, The Rise of the Network Society
  • The rapid expansion of global digital communication infrastructure through the 1990s
  • Research on global financial markets and multinational corporate restructuring
  • Earlier knowledge gap research on unequal access to information and resources
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

The network as social structure

Networks, not fixed hierarchical institutions, became the basic organizing structure of the new economy, culture, and social relations, replacing older, more centralized industrial-era arrangements.

Concept 02

Space of flows versus space of places

Power, capital, and information circulate through global networks relatively detached from geography, while most everyday lived experience remains tied to specific physical places, a structural tension at the heart of the theory.

Concept 03

Network power and the switch

Control over the switches connecting or excluding people, places, and organizations from key global networks becomes a central new axis of power and inequality in the network society.

Examples in practice

Where this pattern turned up

Global financial markets

Capital in the space of flows

Castells's recurring central example: capital moving through electronic networks essentially instantaneously across the globe, increasingly detached from any single national economy or physical trading floor.

Multinational corporations

From hierarchy to network

The shift from older, centralized industrial corporate structures toward flexible, globally networked production and supply chains reorganized how goods and services are actually produced and coordinated worldwide.

Global cities research

Hubs and switches

Certain major financial and media hubs function as key nodes in the network society, concentrating enormous influence, while other regions become comparatively disconnected and marginalized from the same global flows.

Contemporary parallel

The global internet itself

Later scholars frequently describe the internet and social media infrastructure as the most direct, literal contemporary expression of Castells's network society, with platforms functioning as the switches determining who is connected to global information flows and who is not.

The mechanism, demonstrated

Two ways of picturing the same world

The space of flows, and the space of places

Toggle between Castells's two contrasting pictures of the same underlying world, one organized around connected global hubs, the other around a single, fixed local community.

In one sentence
Power in the network society flows through global electronic networks connecting a handful of highly connected hubs, while most everyday life stays rooted in specific places increasingly disconnected from those flows.

The theory asks a reader to notice which spaces they actually inhabit, and which distant, connected hubs might be shaping their circumstances from a place they will never see.

Where the theory ran into trouble

Geography never fully disappeared

The pushback

Critics argued Castells's framework, while capturing something genuinely real about globalization and digital networks, could overstate the novelty and completeness of the shift. Geography, national borders, and physical infrastructure continued to matter enormously even in a supposedly networked, placeless economy, undersea cables and data centers, after all, still sit in specific physical locations.

Some scholars also felt the theory offered more description than guidance, capturing the structure of the network society clearly without saying as much about how ordinary people or excluded communities might meaningfully respond to or resist exclusion from valuable global networks. Later researchers extended and complicated the simple connected versus disconnected binary, finding that degrees and types of connection, not just binary inclusion or exclusion, mattered enormously in practice, a nuance the digital divide research covered earlier in this atlas continues to explore.

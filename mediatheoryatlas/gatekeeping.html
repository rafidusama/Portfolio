Gatekeeping Theory

Kurt Lewin and David Manning White Social psychologist, and a journalism researcher who tested the idea on a real wire editor
Historical context

A theory about grocery shopping, applied to the newsroom

The idea did not start in a newsroom at all. In 1947, the social psychologist Kurt Lewin was studying how families decided what to eat, part of a wartime effort to get Americans to accept less popular but more available cuts of meat and organ meats during rationing. Lewin described food as moving through a series of "channels," with "gates" at certain points controlled by a "gatekeeper," usually the person in the household who did the shopping and cooking, who decided what actually reached the family table.

David Manning White, a journalism researcher, saw the same structure in how news moves from a wire service to a reader. In 1950, he ran what became one of the most cited studies in journalism research. He asked a real wire editor at a small Midwestern newspaper, referred to in the study by the pseudonym Mr. Gates, to save every wire story he rejected over the course of a week, along with a short handwritten note explaining why.

The results were striking. The editor rejected roughly nine out of every ten stories that came across his desk. Some rejections followed conventional news judgment, limited space, stories that duplicated something already run, items too far from the paper's local audience. But a considerable number of his notes revealed something more personal: stories he called uninteresting, or dismissed as one-sided, reflecting his own tastes and political leanings as much as any fixed editorial standard.

White's conclusion reframed how journalism research thought about news itself. The news that reaches an audience is not simply "what happened." It is what survived a long chain of individual decisions, made by people exercising personal judgment at every gate along the way.

What fed this theory
  • Kurt Lewin's 1947 work on channels and gatekeepers in wartime food behavior research
  • David Manning White's 1950 study of a real wire editor, published as "The Gate Keeper"
  • The growth of wire services feeding far more copy to newspapers than any one paper could run
  • Later research extending gatekeeping beyond one editor to organizational and institutional levels
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

The gate

Any point in the flow of news where a decision gets made about whether a piece of information continues on toward the public or stops. A newsroom has many gates, not just one.

Concept 02

The gatekeeper

The person, or later the institution or system, controlling a particular gate. White's study made the point sharply: that person's own taste, politics, and mood shape the decision as much as any formal news standard.

Concept 03

Channels

The pathways information travels through before it reaches an audience, echoing Lewin's original picture of food moving from farm to store to kitchen to table, each stage a chance for something to be added, changed, or stopped.

Examples in practice

Where this pattern turned up

United States, wartime

Lewin's food channel study

Working for the National Research Council during the Second World War, Lewin studied how families accepted or rejected foods like organ meats, finding that the household member who shopped and cooked acted as a gatekeeper deciding what the rest of the family would even have the option to eat.

1949, Midwestern United States

The "Mr. Gates" wire desk study

Over one week, a wire editor rejected the large majority of the stories available to him. His handwritten reasons ranged from ordinary space and duplication concerns to openly subjective judgments about what he found interesting, credible, or worth his readers' time.

Later scholarship

Gatekeeping beyond one editor

Subsequent researchers, notably Pamela Shoemaker and Stephen Reese, expanded the model well beyond a single individual, showing that gates also operate at the level of a news organization's routines, ownership structure, and broader social and political pressures, not just one person's personal taste.

Contemporary parallel

Platforms and algorithmic feeds

Social platforms now perform a version of the same function at enormous scale, with moderation policies and ranking algorithms acting as gates that decide what content most people ever see, replacing one wire editor's handwritten notes with rules and code few readers can inspect directly.

In one sentence
At every point news changes hands, someone decides what passes through the gate, and that decision is never purely mechanical.

The theory does not claim gatekeepers are careless or corrupt. It claims that judgment, personal and institutional both, is baked into news selection at every stage, which means the news an audience receives was never simply "what happened," but what survived the chain of gates in front of it.

Where the theory ran into trouble

One editor's notebook was never the whole newsroom

The pushback

White's original study looked at a single individual's decisions in isolation, which made for a vivid case but a narrow one. Critics pointed out that a wire editor does not operate alone. Ownership pressure, advertiser relationships, professional norms taught in journalism training, and the sheer routines of daily deadline production all shape what reaches a gate in the first place, long before any one editor's personal taste comes into play. Later researchers folded these larger forces back into the model, treating individual judgment as only the most visible layer of a much bigger system.

Digital publishing put pressure on the theory from a different direction. When anyone can post directly to an audience without passing through an editor at all, the very idea of a single, identifiable gate becomes harder to locate, even as new, less visible gates, in the form of platform policies and ranking algorithms, quietly took their place.

Platform Society

José van Dijck, Thomas Poell, and Martijn de Waal Media and communication scholars
Historical context

When the infrastructure itself becomes the story

By the mid-2010s, digital platforms had stopped being a niche technology topic and had become a genuinely society-wide organizing structure, not just for social media but for ride-sharing, food delivery, banking, dating, and dozens of other everyday activities. José van Dijck, together with Thomas Poell and Martijn de Waal, argued in their 2018 book The Platform Society: Public Values in a Connective World that this shift required genuinely new analytical tools, since older media theory had mostly been built to study broadcasters and publishers, not app-based intermediaries sitting between millions of users and each other.

The authors proposed thinking of platforms as a layered ecosystem. Individual, familiar platforms, a particular social media app, a particular ride-sharing service, sit within a deeper infrastructure layer dominated by a small number of very large technology companies whose underlying technical and economic systems much of the rest of the ecosystem quietly depends on, cloud computing, app store rules, payment processing, advertising networks.

Their central argument was that platforms are never neutral technical intermediaries simply connecting users to each other. They actively shape social, economic, and civic life through specific business models, algorithms, and design choices, while frequently presenting themselves publicly as open, neutral infrastructure rather than as powerful economic and social actors making consequential decisions about what content, opportunities, and connections users actually see.

That gap between a platform's public self-description and its actual structural power created what van Dijck and colleagues framed as an urgent tension: core public values like privacy, fairness, and democratic accountability do not automatically align with platforms' profit-driven, engagement-optimizing business models, and protecting those values, they argued, requires active public policy attention rather than simply trusting market competition to resolve the conflict on its own.

What fed this theory
  • Van Dijck, Poell, and de Waal's 2018 book, The Platform Society
  • The rapid expansion of app-based platforms across nearly every sector of daily life
  • Growing public concern over the concentrated power of major technology companies
  • Emerging regulatory debates over platform accountability and data protection
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

The platform ecosystem

Individual platforms operate within a layered structure dominated by infrastructure owned by a small number of very large technology companies, whose systems much of the ecosystem quietly depends on.

Concept 02

Platforms as non-neutral intermediaries

Platforms actively shape social and economic life through business models, algorithms, and design choices, despite often presenting themselves publicly as neutral technical infrastructure.

Concept 03

Public values versus platform logic

Core public values like privacy, fairness, and democratic accountability can conflict with platforms' profit-driven, engagement-optimizing business models, requiring active regulatory attention rather than assuming the market resolves the tension.

Examples in practice

Where this pattern turned up

Social media feeds

Algorithmic content ranking

Van Dijck's central, most direct example of platforms actively shaping what users see through design and algorithmic choices, while often describing themselves publicly as neutral, open spaces for user expression.

The gig economy

Ride-sharing and delivery platforms

Discussed as a parallel case beyond social media, showing how platform logic extends into labor markets, reshaping employment relationships by classifying workers as independent contractors rather than employees.

European Union

Data protection regulation

Cited as an example of public policy actively attempting to reassert public values, particularly privacy and user rights, against platform business logic, illustrating the tension the theory identifies in concrete regulatory form.

Contemporary parallel

Content moderation and antitrust debates

Ongoing debates over platform content moderation policy and antitrust action against major technology companies are frequently framed using van Dijck's platform society concept, treating them as struggles over the same public-values-versus-platform-logic tension.

In one sentence
Digital platforms are not neutral technical pipes connecting people, they actively shape social and economic life through their own business models and algorithms.

The theory asks a reader to look past a platform's self-description as an open, neutral space, and ask instead what business model and design choices are actually determining what they see and who they can reach.

Where the theory ran into trouble

Naming the problem is easier than solving it

The pushback

Critics note the platform society framework, while useful for describing the problem clearly, offers relatively contested and still-evolving guidance on the best regulatory response, with different regions taking substantially different approaches reflecting different values and priorities, and no clear consensus yet on what good platform governance actually looks like in practice. Some scholars also caution against treating platforms as a single homogeneous category, since a social media app, a ride-sharing service, and a cloud infrastructure provider raise genuinely different public value tensions despite sharing structural features.

The framework's own core terms have also had to be continuously revised as the competitive landscape among major technology companies keeps shifting, a reminder that describing a fast-moving structure accurately is an ongoing project rather than a settled conclusion.

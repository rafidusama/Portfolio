Information Disorder

Claire Wardle & Hossein Derakhshan Media researchers, published Information Disorder for the Council of Europe in 2017
Historical context

Two researchers argue misinformation is really three different problems wearing one name

Claire Wardle and Hossein Derakhshan published Information Disorder in 2017, at a moment when misinformation had become the word everyone used for almost any kind of false or misleading content online, a flattening they argued was making the problem harder, not easier, to actually address.

Their framework split the single word into three, sorted along two axes: whether the content is false, and whether the person sharing it intends to cause harm. Misinformation is false information shared without intent to harm, someone genuinely believing and passing on something untrue. Disinformation is false information deliberately created and shared to cause harm. Malinformation is genuine, factually accurate information deliberately shared to cause harm, a leaked private document or an out-of-context true fact weaponized against someone.

The distinction mattered practically, not just semantically. A platform policy or media literacy campaign built to fight deliberate disinformation campaigns will miss most misinformation, which spreads through ordinary people sharing something they sincerely believe, and neither approach touches malinformation, where the content is entirely true and only the intent behind sharing it is harmful.

Wardle and Derakhshan also mapped the life cycle of a piece of disordered information, from creation through production and distribution, arguing effective intervention has to target the right stage, since content already viral is far harder to stop than content still being seeded.

What fed this theory
  • The 2016 U.S. presidential election and Brexit referendum, both marked by intense public debate over fake news
  • Earlier propaganda and computational propaganda research documenting deliberate, coordinated falsehoods
  • Platform companies' own struggles to write moderation policy under the single, imprecise umbrella term fake news
  • Journalistic and NGO fact-checking organizations' need for a more precise operational vocabulary
  • The Council of Europe's interest in a policy-ready framework distinct from purely academic misinformation research
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

Misinformation

False information shared by someone who believes it to be true and has no intention of causing harm, the most common but least discussed category of the three.

Concept 02

Disinformation

False information created and deliberately shared with the intent to cause harm, the category most associated with coordinated propaganda and computational propaganda campaigns.

Concept 03

Malinformation

Genuine, accurate information deliberately shared out of its proper context, or shared specifically to cause harm, a category most misinformation frameworks before this one had no clear place for.

Examples in practice

Where this idea was put to work

Ongoing

A well-meaning relative's shared health claim

Someone who genuinely believes a false home remedy and shares it to help family members is producing misinformation, not disinformation, a distinction that changes what kind of intervention, correction rather than deterrence, actually helps.

2016

Coordinated election-related fabrications

Fabricated stories created and distributed specifically to influence the 2016 U.S. election and Brexit vote are the framework's clearest disinformation case, false content built deliberately to cause harm.

Ongoing

Leaked private messages published out of context

A genuine, unaltered document or message, shared publicly specifically to damage someone despite being entirely accurate, is the framework's clearest example of malinformation.

Platform policy

Content moderation categories

Major platforms have gradually adopted versions of this three-part distinction in their own policy language, since a single fake news category cannot meaningfully distinguish an honest mistake from a coordinated harassment campaign.

Where the idea still shows up

The vocabulary behind every serious fact-checking operation now

Journalism and platform trust and safety teams have largely adopted this three-part vocabulary as their working standard, replacing the vaguer fake news label that dominated coverage before 2017.

The framework also clarified why media literacy campaigns built only to spot deliberate lies were never going to solve the whole problem, since most disordered information people actually encounter is misinformation, shared sincerely by people who believe it.

Why this matters for media literacy
  • It gives journalists and platforms a precise, policy-ready vocabulary that replaced the imprecise term fake news
  • It clarifies why Computational Propaganda, elsewhere in this atlas, describes only part of the larger information disorder problem
  • Its life cycle model shapes where platforms and fact-checkers now try to intervene, closer to the point of creation rather than after virality

In one sentence
Fake news is grossly inadequate to describe the complexity of the problem.

Wardle and Derakhshan's founding objection. Three different problems, an honest mistake, a deliberate lie, and a weaponized truth, need three different vocabularies and three different responses, not one blunt label.

Where the theory ran into trouble

The pushback

The pushback

Critics note the falseness and intent axes are not always easy to determine in practice, especially intent, which is rarely stated outright and often has to be inferred from circumstantial evidence.

Some researchers argue the framework, built for policy use, sacrifices some analytical precision for practical clarity, and that real cases frequently blur across more than one category at once.

Even with those limits, the three-part distinction has become the standard reference point in journalism, platform policy, and academic research alike, replacing the far vaguer language that preceded it almost entirely.

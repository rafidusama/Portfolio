1970s Political Economy

Knowledge Gap Hypothesis

Phillip Tichenor, George Donohue, and Clarice Olien Communication researchers, University of Minnesota
Historical context

More information for everyone did not mean equal information for everyone

Through the 1950s and 1960s, a hopeful assumption ran through a great deal of communication and development research, including much of the diffusion and modernization work covered earlier in this atlas: if you simply increased the flow of information into a community, everyone in it would eventually catch up to everyone else. More newspapers, more broadcasts, more public affairs coverage would, in this view, act as a great leveler.

In 1970, three researchers at the University of Minnesota, Phillip Tichenor, George Donohue, and Clarice Olien, published a paper that quietly dismantled that assumption. Studying how public affairs and science information spread through communities as media coverage increased, they found something close to the opposite of what the leveling theory predicted. Segments of the population with higher socioeconomic status, more formal education, more money, more relevant social contacts, tended to acquire new information faster than lower socioeconomic segments, even when everyone technically had the same access to the same newspapers and broadcasts.

The result, they argued, was that the gap in knowledge between these groups tended to widen, not narrow, as media coverage of an issue increased. More information flowing into a community did not raise everyone equally. It tended to lift those who already had the most resources for processing and using that information fastest, stretching the distance between them and everyone else.

The finding mattered well beyond academic communication research. It cut directly against the optimistic assumption behind a great deal of public health and civic information campaigns, and behind much of the development communication tradition, since it suggested that simply broadcasting more information was not, on its own, a neutral or equalizing act.

What fed this theory
  • The 1970 University of Minnesota paper, "Mass Media Flow and Differential Growth in Knowledge"
  • Public affairs and science media coverage of the era, unevenly absorbed across audiences
  • Growing skepticism toward the optimistic assumptions behind development communication research
  • Later fieldwork by the same researchers on local community conflict and information spread
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

Differential knowledge acquisition

Higher socioeconomic groups tend to pick up mass media information faster, thanks to stronger existing background knowledge, better communication skills, and social networks more likely to reinforce and discuss what they read or hear.

Concept 02

The widening gap

The theory's central and most provocative claim: increasing the flow of media information into a community tends to widen, rather than close, the knowledge gap between higher and lower socioeconomic groups.

Concept 03

Ceiling effects and local conditions

Later research by the same team found conditions, particularly high local interest or conflict in a small, close-knit community, could narrow the gap instead, since there is only so much anyone can know about a topic before hitting a natural ceiling.

Examples in practice

Where this pattern turned up

Minnesota, 1970

The original public affairs study

Tichenor, Donohue, and Olien's founding research tracked how print-heavy science and public affairs coverage disproportionately benefited more educated readers, establishing the core widening-gap pattern the theory is named for.

Public health campaigns

Uneven uptake of health information

Public health information campaigns have repeatedly been found to widen gaps between more and less educated audiences, unless specifically designed with targeted, localized outreach aimed at lower socioeconomic groups rather than a single broad broadcast approach.

Community conflict research

When the gap narrows instead

In later fieldwork, the same research team found that small, homogeneous communities experiencing high local conflict or interest around an issue sometimes saw the knowledge gap narrow rather than widen, an important qualifier to the original, broader hypothesis.

Contemporary parallel

The digital divide

Research into internet and smartphone access has documented the same basic pattern in a new medium: those with more resources, skills, and existing knowledge tend to benefit disproportionately from new information technology, widening rather than closing longstanding gaps.

In one sentence
More media coverage of an issue tends to widen the knowledge gap between socioeconomic groups, not close it, unless local conditions push against that pattern.

The theory complicates a comforting assumption behind a lot of public information efforts, that simply making information available is enough. Who actually absorbs it, and how quickly, depends heavily on resources most campaigns never account for.

Where the theory ran into trouble

A real pattern, but not an inevitable one

The pushback

While the widening gap pattern has been documented repeatedly, it is not universal, and researchers, including the original authors, found it interacts heavily with local conditions such as community size, social conflict, and how directly relevant an issue feels to a particular group. Critics have also argued that socioeconomic status alone is too blunt a variable, and that factors like personal motivation, media literacy skills, and the strength of someone's social network may matter just as much in explaining who actually absorbs new information quickly.

The rise of the internet complicated the picture further by both testing and confirming the theory's resilience. Early hopes that universal internet access would finally deliver the equalizing effect earlier media never managed have run into the same basic dynamic Tichenor, Donohue, and Olien described decades earlier, now studied under the label of the digital divide, suggesting the underlying pattern is less about any one medium and more about the resources people bring to whatever medium is in front of them.

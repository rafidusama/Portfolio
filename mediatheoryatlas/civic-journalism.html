Public and Civic Journalism

Jay Rosen, with practitioners including Davis Merritt Journalism professor, New York University, and a longtime newspaper editor
Historical context

Asking journalism to stop watching democracy from the sidelines

By the late 1980s, a growing number of American editors and journalism scholars had become worried about something conventional professional standards seemed poorly equipped to address: declining public trust in journalism, alongside falling civic engagement more broadly. Election coverage in particular had settled into a familiar pattern, treating campaigns mainly as a strategic contest between competing insiders, a horse race of tactics and polling, with ordinary citizens cast as spectators watching from the stands rather than participants in a shared democratic life.

Davis Merritt, editor of the Wichita Eagle, put a different approach into direct practice in 1990 with a project called "Your Vote Counts," deliberately organizing election coverage around the issues and questions Wichita voters themselves identified as important, rather than exclusively following the campaigns' own strategic messaging. The experiment became one of the founding practical case studies of what came to be called public or civic journalism.

Jay Rosen, a journalism professor at New York University, became the movement's leading academic voice, arguing that journalism's traditional ideal of pure detachment and neutrality had itself become a kind of civic failure. In Rosen's view, a healthy press could not simply report on democracy from a safe distance. It bore some real responsibility for the health of public deliberation itself, actively helping citizens engage with shared problems rather than treating engagement as somebody else's job.

Through the 1990s, foundation funding, notably from the Pew Center for Civic Journalism, supported dozens of similar experiments at local newsrooms across the country, from community forums to coverage explicitly organized around citizen-identified concerns rather than official political agendas.

Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

Citizens as participants, not spectators

Reframing the audience as active participants in democratic life who deserve genuine engagement, rather than as an audience simply watching political conflict unfold from a distance.

Concept 02

Journalism's civic responsibility

Journalists, in this view, bear some real responsibility for the health of public deliberation and community problem-solving, not just for producing neutral, detached reporting.

Concept 03

Public listening

Deliberately organizing coverage around the issues and questions citizens themselves identify as important, often through community forums, rather than exclusively following political insiders' own strategic agenda.

Examples in practice

Where this pattern turned up

Wichita, 1990

"Your Vote Counts"

Davis Merritt's foundational practical experiment, organizing election coverage around voter-identified issues rather than campaign strategy, widely cited as one of the movement's originating case studies.

Charlotte, 1993

"Taking Back Our Neighborhoods"

The Charlotte Observer used community listening sessions and public forums to shape its crime coverage, another frequently cited early civic journalism project applying the same core approach to a different local issue.

1990s, nationwide

The Pew Center for Civic Journalism

A foundation-funded initiative that supported and studied dozens of local civic journalism experiments across American newsrooms through the decade, helping formalize the movement's practices and evidence base.

Contemporary parallel

Engaged journalism today

Modern newsroom initiatives around reader surveys, audience-driven coverage priorities, and dedicated engagement editor roles are frequently described as direct descendants of civic journalism's core arguments, even where the original label has largely faded from use.

In one sentence
Journalism shouldn't just report on democracy from a neutral distance, this movement argued, it should actively help citizens deliberate about shared problems.

The theory challenges a reader to notice whether coverage of a public issue is organized around what officials and campaigns want to say, or around the questions ordinary people affected by that issue actually have.

Where the theory ran into trouble

A line between reporting and advocacy, tested

The pushback

Critics, including prominent editors and journalists, argued civic journalism risked crossing an important line between reporting and advocacy, blurring the traditional separation between observing events and actively trying to shape civic outcomes, long considered a core tenet of professional journalistic objectivity. Some also questioned whether foundation-funded civic journalism projects sometimes favored feel-good community engagement over the harder, more adversarial accountability reporting that might actually upset local power structures.

By the early 2000s, dedicated funding and momentum for the movement as a distinct named initiative had faded considerably. Even so, many of its core arguments, listening to audience concerns, involving communities directly in coverage decisions, focusing on solutions rather than only problems, persisted and resurfaced under different labels in later journalism reform efforts, suggesting the underlying critique outlived the specific movement that first articulated it.

News Values

Johan Galtung & Mari Holmboe Ruge Peace researcher and sociologist, published The Structure of Foreign News in 1965
Historical context

Norwegian researchers ask why some crises make the news and others don't

Johan Galtung and Mari Holmboe Ruge set out in 1965 to answer a question editors make instinctively, dozens of times a day, but had rarely tried to state as an explicit theory: out of everything that happens in the world on a given day, what determines which events become news at all?

Studying coverage of foreign crises in Norwegian newspapers, they proposed a set of factors, later expanded by other researchers, that make an event more likely to be selected and, once selected, more likely to be distorted in a predictable direction. Their original list included frequency, threshold, unambiguity, meaningfulness, consonance, unexpectedness, continuity, composition, reference to elite nations, reference to elite people, personalization, and negativity.

Their deeper claim was structural rather than moralizing. Journalists are not villains cynically distorting the world; they are working under real constraints, deadlines, space, and audience attention, and these values are the practical heuristics that let a newsroom sort an overwhelming stream of possible stories into a workable, finite news hole every single day.

The theory's sharpest edge is what it implies about distortion: an event high on several of these factors at once, sudden, negative, involving an elite country, easily personalized, becomes vastly more likely to run than an equally important but slow-moving, ambiguous, structural story, whatever its underlying significance.

What fed this theory
  • Galtung's parallel work in peace and conflict research, which shaped his interest in how war coverage gets selected
  • Cold War-era foreign correspondence, dominated by a handful of wire services and Western newsrooms
  • Earlier gatekeeping research by Kurt Lewin and David Manning White on the individual editor's selection process
  • The practical, deadline-driven constraints of print newsroom space in the 1960s
  • Later communication scholars who expanded the original list to include factors like visual quality and this-is-happening-now immediacy
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

Threshold and amplitude

An event needs to cross a minimum threshold of scale or drama to register at all, and the more dramatically it exceeds that threshold, the more prominent the resulting coverage.

Concept 02

Negativity and unexpectedness

Bad news and surprising news travel further than good, expected news, not because journalists prefer bad news but because both properties make an event stand out against the routine background.

Concept 03

Elite reference and personalization

Events involving powerful nations, powerful people, or a story that can be told through a single identifiable individual are systematically more likely to run than equally significant events lacking any of those hooks.

Examples in practice

Where this idea was put to work

1960s foreign desks

Galtung and Ruge's original Norwegian case study

Their analysis of Norwegian newspaper coverage of Congo, Cuba, and Cyprus crises found coverage patterns closely tracking the value list, elite-nation involvement and dramatic negativity predicting selection more reliably than a crisis's actual human toll.

Ongoing

Natural disaster coverage

A disaster in a wealthy, elite-referenced country routinely receives dramatically more coverage than a larger disaster in a country with fewer of these news values attached, a pattern researchers have documented repeatedly since the original study.

Ongoing

The single rescued person versus the statistical crisis

A single trapped individual pulled from rubble regularly outdraws coverage of a slow-moving famine affecting millions, the personalization and unambiguity of the first story outweighing the far larger scale of the second.

Contemporary platforms

Engagement metrics as a new elite reference

Some scholars argue platform algorithms have effectively added a new, thirteenth news value, measurable early engagement, that now competes with, and sometimes overrides, the traditional editorial list entirely.

Where the idea still shows up

The checklist every editor still runs, whether or not they'd call it that

Editors rarely cite Galtung and Ruge by name, but nearly every debate about why one story ran above the fold and another got buried is, underneath, an argument about which of these values a competing story satisfied more completely.

The theory also supplies one of the sharper critiques available of algorithmic news feeds, which scholars increasingly argue have simply automated and intensified negativity, unexpectedness, and personalization as selection criteria, without any of the editorial judgment that once tempered them.

Why this matters for media literacy
  • It gives Gatekeeping Theory, elsewhere in this atlas, its specific working criteria for what makes something worth selecting
  • Its vocabulary, threshold, elite reference, personalization, remains standard in newsroom and journalism-school training
  • It supplies one of the clearest theoretical tools for critiquing what engagement-optimized algorithms have automated

In one sentence
The more events satisfy the criteria, the more likely they will become news.

The authors' own summary, deliberately additive. No single value guarantees coverage, but each one satisfied raises the odds, and a story stacking several at once becomes close to impossible for a newsroom to ignore.

Where the theory ran into trouble

The pushback

The pushback

Critics have long argued the list, developed from a study of just three Cold War-era foreign crises, was never tested rigorously enough to justify how widely it has since been applied across very different media systems and eras.

Some scholars argue the framework describes newsroom output more than it explains the psychological or organizational process that actually produces it, closer to a naming of patterns than a causal theory of why editors behave this way.

Even with those limits, later expansions of the list have kept it the most durable working vocabulary in journalism studies for describing what makes one event more reportable than another, updated repeatedly but never seriously replaced.

Diffusion of Innovations

Everett Rogers Rural sociologist, Ohio State University and later Michigan State University
Historical context

A theory built in cornfields, not newsrooms

This theory did not begin with mass media at all. It began with an Iowa State College study asking a narrower question: why did it take some farmers years longer than others to start planting a new, higher-yield hybrid seed corn that was, by every measurable standard, better than what they already used? Bryce Ryan and Neal Gross tracked the answer across the 1930s and published their findings in 1943, and the pattern they found became the seed, quite literally, of a much bigger idea.

Everett Rogers, working in the same rural sociology tradition, spent years gathering similar studies from agriculture, medicine, and education, all describing the same shape: a new idea or product spreads through a population slowly at first, picks up speed once a critical mass adopts it, and then tails off as only a small, reluctant group remains unconvinced. In 1962, Rogers pulled this pattern together in his book Diffusion of Innovations, turning a farming case study into a general theory of how any new idea moves through any social system.

One of Rogers's clearest supporting studies, run by James Coleman, Elihu Katz, and Herbert Menzel, tracked how doctors in four small Illinois towns adopted a new antibiotic in the mid-1950s. It found the same S-shaped adoption curve, and confirmed that doctors leaned heavily on conversations with colleagues they respected professionally before prescribing the new drug themselves, a clear echo of the opinion leadership already described in Two-Step Flow.

Where Two-Step Flow asked how a message spreads through a social network, Diffusion of Innovations asks how long that spread actually takes, and what determines whether it accelerates or stalls before reaching everyone.

What fed this theory
  • The 1943 Iowa hybrid seed corn study by Bryce Ryan and Neal Gross
  • The 1966 Illinois drug adoption study by Coleman, Katz, and Menzel
  • Post-war agricultural extension services trying to speed up farming innovation
  • Rogers's own fieldwork across agriculture, medicine, and education research
  • The opinion leadership concept already established by Two-Step Flow
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

The adopter categories

Rogers split any population into five groups by how early they take up an innovation: innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority, and laggards, each with a distinct size and social profile.

Concept 02

The S-curve

Plotted over time, cumulative adoption traces a slow start, a steep middle climb once the early majority joins in, and a flattening tail as only the most reluctant adopters remain.

Concept 03

Perceived attributes of innovation

Five qualities determine how fast something spreads: its advantage over what it replaces, its fit with existing habits, how complex it is to understand, whether it can be tried on a small scale first, and how visible its results are to others.

Examples in practice

Where this curve turned up

Iowa, 1928 to 1941

Hybrid seed corn

It took about thirteen years for the hybrid seed to go from a handful of adventurous farmers to near universal use across the surveyed county, even though the yield advantage was clear from the start. Most farmers waited to see a few trusted, respected neighbors succeed with it first.

Illinois, mid-1950s

A new antibiotic among doctors

Coleman, Katz, and Menzel tracked how quickly physicians in four towns began prescribing a new drug. Doctors who were more professionally connected and more frequently consulted by peers adopted it earlier, and their adoption visibly pulled others along behind them.

United States, 1948 to 1960

Television ownership

Household television ownership in the United States followed a textbook S-curve across the 1950s: a small share of households in 1948, a steep climb through the middle of the decade, and near saturation by 1960, following almost exactly the shape Rogers's theory predicts for any successful innovation.

Development communication

Family planning and public health campaigns

Rogers later applied the same framework directly to development programs in Asia, Africa, and Latin America, from contraceptive adoption to boiling drinking water, treating each as a diffusion problem with its own adopter categories and trusted local go-betweens.

In one sentence
A new idea spreads slowly at first, quickly once enough trusted people adopt it, then slowly again as only the most reluctant remain.

The theory treats adoption as a social process, not a simple response to the innovation's own merits. How good something is matters less than how visible, testable, and compatible with existing habits it appears to the people watching their neighbors decide first.

Where the theory ran into trouble

A curve built for farms does not fit everything

The pushback

Critics pointed out that Rogers's original research base leaned heavily on innovations that were, in hindsight, genuinely beneficial: better seed, better medicine, better sanitation. That built an optimistic assumption into the model, that diffusion is generally a good thing that should be sped up, which does not hold nearly as well when the thing spreading is a harmful rumor, a risky financial product, or a piece of disinformation moving through the same social channels.

The theory was also developed almost entirely around close-knit, geographically bounded communities, like a single Iowa county or four Illinois towns, where opinion leaders were easy to identify because everyone more or less knew everyone. Large, loosely connected online networks scatter that structure considerably, making "who counts as an opinion leader" a much harder question than it was for Rogers's original farmers and physicians.

Attention Economy

Herbert Simon; later Tim Wu & Michael Goldhaber Economist and Nobel laureate; concept later developed by media critics through the platform era
Historical context

An economist notices information has a strange side effect

In 1971, the economist and Nobel laureate Herbert Simon made an observation that would take four decades to become common sense: in an information-rich world, information consumes something else, namely the attention of its recipients, and a wealth of information therefore creates a poverty of attention along with a need to allocate that attention efficiently among the sources competing for it.

Simon was writing before the internet existed, mostly concerned with organizational decision-making, but his framing, attention as the genuinely scarce resource, information as the abundant one, inverted the assumption behind most earlier media theory, which had worried about scarce information reaching a passive audience rather than abundant information competing for a limited audience.

The concept sat mostly dormant in economics until platforms built explicitly around maximizing time-on-site and engagement metrics made Simon's observation into a literal business model. Media critic Tim Wu's 2016 book The Attention Merchants traced the long commercial history, from penny newspapers through radio, television, and finally digital platforms, of industries built specifically around capturing and reselling human attention to advertisers.

Michael Goldhaber and others pushed the idea further, arguing attention itself functions as a currency in the digital economy, complete with its own scarcity, its own forms of accumulation, and its own asymmetries of power between those who command large audiences and those competing for a sliver of anyone's notice.

What fed this theory
  • Herbert Simon's 1971 essay on organizations operating in information-rich environments
  • The penny press's earlier commercial model of selling audience attention to advertisers rather than content to readers
  • The rise of engagement-optimized platform algorithms through the 2010s
  • Behavioral psychology research on variable reward schedules, later applied deliberately to app and feed design
  • Growing public concern over smartphone and social media addiction and its effect on sustained attention
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

Attention as scarce resource

Simon's founding inversion: in a world flooded with information, the bottleneck is not access to content but the finite human attention available to consume it, making attention, not information, the thing worth competing for.

Concept 02

Attention merchants

Wu's term for businesses whose core product is not the content they distribute but the audience attention that content captures, which they then resell, typically to advertisers.

Concept 03

Engagement optimization

The deliberate design of feeds, notifications, and recommendation systems to maximize time spent and interactions generated, treating attention capture as a measurable, optimizable engineering target rather than a side effect of good content.

Examples in practice

Where this idea was put to work

1830s

The penny press

Early American newspapers that cut prices to near zero, funding themselves instead by selling readers' attention to advertisers, represent, in Wu's account, the attention economy's true commercial origin point.

1950s and 1960s

Commercial television ratings

The Nielsen ratings system turned audience attention into a directly tradable commodity, with advertising rates set almost entirely by how many eyeballs a given time slot could reliably capture.

2000s onward

The infinite scroll and autoplay

Interface design decisions like autoplay and removing natural stopping points were built explicitly to keep attention captured past the point a user might otherwise have paused and left.

Contemporary platforms

The engagement-optimized feed algorithm

Recommendation systems trained to maximize watch time and interaction rates are perhaps the purest contemporary expression of the attention economy, treating human notice as the raw material the entire business runs on.

Where the idea still shows up

The economic logic underneath the algorithmic feed

Almost every debate about doomscrolling, notification fatigue, or phone addiction is implicitly an argument about the attention economy, whether or not the speaker uses Simon's or Wu's vocabulary.

Journalism itself has been reshaped by the same logic, with headline writing, story selection, and publishing cadence increasingly influenced by what will capture attention within a platform's algorithm, not simply what is most newsworthy by traditional editorial judgment.

Why this matters for media literacy
  • It supplies the working vocabulary, attention merchants, engagement, now standard in tech criticism and platform studies
  • It directly informs contemporary debates about algorithmic curation, covered elsewhere in this atlas under Filter Bubble Theory
  • It connects nineteenth-century advertising history to twenty-first-century platform design as a single continuous business model

In one sentence
A wealth of information creates a poverty of attention.

Herbert Simon's 1971 line, written decades before the platforms that would make it a business model. Information is no longer scarce; the human capacity to attend to it is, and that scarcity is what gets bought and sold.

Where the theory ran into trouble

The pushback

The pushback

Critics note that treating attention as a simple, fungible resource risks flattening enormous differences in what different kinds of attention, focused reading versus reflexive scrolling, actually accomplish for the person giving it.

Some economists question whether attention behaves like a genuine market commodity at all, since it cannot be stored, transferred, or measured with anything like the precision the metaphor implies.

Even skeptics of the strict economic framing generally accept the underlying behavioral claim: platforms designed around engagement metrics measurably do change how people spend their time, whatever the right theoretical vocabulary for describing that change turns out to be.

Mediatization

Stig Hjarvard, Friedrich Krotz & Andreas Hepp European media scholars, developed mediatization theory across the 1990s and 2000s
Historical context

A papal funeral run on a broadcaster's timetable

Mediatization theory grew out of a Scandinavian and German tradition of media research asking a question distinct from most earlier effects theories: not what media do to individual audience members, but what happens to entire institutions, politics, religion, family life, once they can no longer function without accounting for media logic.

Stig Hjarvard argued that institutions increasingly become dependent on media and, over time, adapt their own internal organization to media's rhythms and formats, a papal funeral choreographed with television cameras in mind, a legislature timing announcements to evening news deadlines, a political campaign built around debate formats rather than town halls.

Friedrich Krotz pushed the concept further with the idea of mediatization as a meta-process, comparable in scale to globalization or individualization, a long historical arc in which more and more domains of social life become entangled with, and reshaped by, communication media.

Andreas Hepp and others later introduced deep mediatization to describe the most recent phase, in which digital, datafied, and algorithmically organized media are not just one influence among many on social institutions but have become the infrastructure through which nearly all social relationships, coordination, and even self-understanding now pass.

What fed this theory
  • Scandinavian and German media sociology's interest in institutional, rather than purely individual, media effects
  • McLuhan's earlier medium theory, extended from cognition to institutional structure
  • The professionalization of political communication around broadcast news formats and deadlines
  • The shift from broadcast to digital, datafied media environments in the 2000s and 2010s
  • Debates within European media sociology about whether mediatization names a genuinely new process or simply repackages older ideas
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

Media logic

The particular formats, formal constraints, and commercial incentives, deadlines, soundbite length, visual drama, native to a media system, which institutions increasingly design their own activities around satisfying.

Concept 02

Institutional dependency

Politics, religion, sport, and family life become structurally reliant on media exposure to function effectively, reshaping their internal timing, language, and priorities to fit media's requirements.

Concept 03

Deep mediatization

Hepp's term for the most recent stage, in which digital and datafied media are not an external influence on institutions but the basic infrastructure institutions now operate through, making the earlier idea of media as separate from social life increasingly hard to sustain.

Examples in practice

Where this idea was put to work

Contemporary

A papal funeral

Modern papal funerals are explicitly staged with global television broadcast in mind, timed and choreographed for maximum visual and ceremonial clarity on camera, a religious institution reorganizing its own ritual around media logic.

Political communication

The rise of the sound bite

Legislative debate and campaign messaging increasingly compress arguments into broadcast-friendly lengths, reshaping political speech itself around the formal constraints of television and, later, social media.

Sport

Match schedules set by broadcast deals

Kickoff times for major sporting events are frequently set to fit international broadcast windows rather than local convenience, a sport reorganizing its own calendar around media logic.

Digital era

Political campaigns built around platforms

Contemporary campaigns increasingly design events and messaging specifically for platform virality, reflecting deep mediatization's claim that digital infrastructure now shapes institutional behavior from the inside.

Where the idea still shows up

The concept behind why everything now happens on a content schedule

Newsroom debates about publishing to a platform's algorithm rather than a print deadline are a textbook case of mediatization in action, an institution reorganizing its internal workflow around a media system's formal requirements.

Deep mediatization is increasingly the framework scholars reach for to describe how datafied platforms do not simply carry political, religious, or educational activity but actively restructure how those institutions plan, measure, and understand their own work.

Why this matters for media literacy
  • It supplies the vocabulary, media logic, deep mediatization, now standard in European media sociology
  • It connects earlier medium theory to contemporary platform studies without collapsing the two together
  • It gives journalism scholars language for describing how newsroom workflow itself is reshaped by platform requirements

In one sentence
Media have become an integral part of other social institutions, while also acquiring the status of social institutions in their own right.

Hjarvard's summary of the core claim. Media are not a separate channel institutions occasionally use; they are increasingly the infrastructure other institutions are built on top of.

Where the theory ran into trouble

The pushback

The pushback

Critics argue mediatization sometimes describes a process so broad it becomes difficult to test or falsify, closer to a general historical narrative than a specific, checkable theory.

Some media sociologists have questioned whether mediatization names anything genuinely new, or whether it repackages older observations from medium theory and political communication research under a fashionable label.

Even skeptics generally concede that the concept of deep mediatization usefully captures something distinct about the datafied, platform-dependent present, where the older idea of media as one influence among several on otherwise independent institutions has become much harder to defend.

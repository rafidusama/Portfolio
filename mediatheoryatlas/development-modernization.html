Development and Modernization Theory

Daniel Lerner and Wilbur Schramm Communication researchers working on postwar and postcolonial development programs
Historical context

Media as a tool for building modern nations

After the Second World War, dozens of new nations emerged from colonial rule across Asia, Africa, and the Middle East, and both Cold War superpowers were eager to shape how those nations developed. Communication researchers were drawn directly into that project, asking a very practical question: could mass media actually speed up a country's economic and social development.

Daniel Lerner spent the early 1950s interviewing villagers across Turkey, Lebanon, Iran, Egypt, and Jordan, and published his findings in 1958 as The Passing of Traditional Society. He argued that exposure to media, radio broadcasts, newspapers, and eventually film and television, cultivated what he called psychic mobility or empathy, the capacity to imagine oneself living a different, more modern kind of life than the one a person was born into. That imaginative leap, Lerner argued, was often the first real step toward the more visible markers of development: literacy, urbanization, and political participation.

Wilbur Schramm took the argument further in a 1964 report for UNESCO, Mass Media and National Development. He described media as a kind of magic multiplier, a tool that, once deployed deliberately by newly independent governments and development agencies, could accelerate literacy campaigns, agricultural modernization, and public health outreach far faster than these changes would occur on their own.

Much of this research shared funding, personnel, and assumptions with Diffusion of Innovations. Everett Rogers, Lerner, and Schramm all moved in overlapping academic and policy circles, and it is not a coincidence that a theory about how new farming techniques spread through a community and a theory about how whole nations modernize both leaned on the same basic picture: media exposure as the engine, and traditional habits as the friction it needed to overcome.

What fed this theory
  • Daniel Lerner's 1958 fieldwork across the Middle East, published as The Passing of Traditional Society
  • Wilbur Schramm's 1964 UNESCO report, Mass Media and National Development
  • Postwar decolonization and the emergence of dozens of newly independent nations
  • Cold War competition for influence over how those nations would develop
  • Shared researchers and funding with the diffusion of innovations tradition
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

Psychic mobility

Lerner's term for the imaginative capacity to picture oneself in a different, more modern social role, which he believed media exposure cultivated even before any material change occurred.

Concept 02

The magic multiplier

Schramm's metaphor for media's presumed power to speed up development outcomes, treating radio and print not as a byproduct of a modern society but as a tool for actively building one.

Concept 03

Linear modernization stages

A society moves along a single path from traditional to transitional to modern, with media exposure treated as one of the main forces pulling a population from one stage to the next.

Examples in practice

Where this framework was put to work

Middle East, early 1950s

Lerner's village interviews

Lerner and his research team asked villagers to imagine themselves as a newspaper editor or a national leader. Villagers with more exposure to radio and print could imagine these unfamiliar roles far more readily than those with little media contact, a finding Lerner treated as evidence of psychic mobility in action.

India, 1950s to 1960s

Radio farm forums

Group radio listening sessions, followed by structured discussion among farmers, were used to spread new agricultural techniques across rural India, a model directly combining Schramm's development communication approach with the opinion leadership ideas from diffusion research.

UNESCO, early 1960s

Minimum media standards

UNESCO reports from this period proposed benchmark levels of newspaper circulation, radio receivers, and cinema seats per capita as indicators nations should aim for, treating media access itself as a measurable rung on the ladder toward development.

South Asia, 1970s onward

Agricultural and family planning broadcasts

Newly independent nations across South Asia, including Bangladesh, built radio and later television programming explicitly aimed at agricultural extension and family planning, following the same development communication logic Lerner and Schramm had laid out a decade or two earlier.

In one sentence
Media exposure was treated not just as a sign of a developing society, but as one of the forces actively building it.

The theory gave governments and development agencies a clear justification for investing heavily in radio, print, and later television as tools of national policy, not merely as entertainment or information services.

Where the theory ran into trouble

One ladder, built with one endpoint in mind

The pushback

From the 1970s onward, critics argued the entire framework quietly treated Western industrial societies as the single, universal destination of development, measuring every other culture's progress by how closely it came to resembling that one model. This made "traditional" practices look like obstacles to be overcome rather than valid ways of organizing a society, and it had little to say about the colonial history and global economic structures that had shaped which nations were "developed" and which were not in the first place.

Herbert Schiller and other political economy researchers picked up this thread directly, arguing that the flow of media, technology, and cultural content from wealthy nations to poorer ones looked less like neutral development assistance and more like a new form of cultural influence following the old routes of colonial power. That critique became its own theory later in this atlas.

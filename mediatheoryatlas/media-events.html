Media Events

Daniel Dayan & Elihu Katz Communication scholars, published Media Events: The Live Broadcasting of History in 1992
Historical context

A royal wedding stops an entire country at the same moment

Daniel Dayan and Elihu Katz noticed something television news coverage did not fully capture: certain broadcasts, a royal wedding, a moon landing, a papal funeral, a presidential debate, were not just reported on but experienced as shared, ceremonial occasions, watched live by enormous audiences who had cleared their schedules specifically to watch together.

They called these media events, and insisted on a strict definition: pre-planned, not spontaneous news; broadcast live; interrupting the normal television schedule; and organized around a script the broadcaster and the event's organizers essentially agree to follow together, honoring rather than merely covering the occasion.

Their framework sorted media events into three genres, borrowed loosely from classical narrative structure: Contests, like elections and championship sports, built around uncertain competitive outcomes; Conquests, like the moon landing, built around a heroic achievement; and Coronations, like royal weddings and state funerals, built around solemn, ceremonial transitions.

The deeper claim was sociological, drawing on Emile Durkheim's idea of collective effervescence: watching a media event together, even from separate living rooms, produces something like the social bonding effect of a shared religious ritual, temporarily knitting a fragmented mass audience into something that feels, for a few hours, like a single community.

What fed this theory
  • Television's mid-century capacity for genuinely live, simultaneous national broadcast
  • Emile Durkheim's sociology of collective effervescence and shared ritual
  • High-profile ceremonial broadcasts, royal weddings, moon landings, that Dayan and Katz treated as their core case studies
  • Elihu Katz's earlier work on the two-step flow, giving him a long-standing interest in how mass audiences actually experience broadcast content
  • The Cold War-era symbolic weight placed on live, globally watched technological and ceremonial milestones
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

Pre-planned interruption

A media event is scheduled in advance and deliberately interrupts ordinary broadcasting, distinguishing it from breaking news, which is unplanned by definition.

Concept 02

Contests, Conquests, Coronations

Dayan and Katz's three narrative genres for classifying media events, each organized around a different kind of dramatic tension, competitive, heroic, or ceremonial.

Concept 03

Collective effervescence

Durkheim's term, borrowed by Dayan and Katz, for the shared emotional charge a large audience experiences watching the same ritual moment simultaneously, even while physically apart.

Examples in practice

Where this idea was put to work

1969

The Apollo 11 moon landing

Dayan and Katz's paradigm case of a Conquest: a live, globally watched technological achievement broadcast as a shared, historic moment rather than merely reported after the fact.

1981

The wedding of Charles and Diana

A Coronation-type event, watched by hundreds of millions worldwide, organized around ceremony and continuity rather than competition or achievement.

U.S. presidential elections

Election night coverage

A recurring Contest-type media event, its uncertain outcome and live vote-counting drama built specifically for interrupted, simultaneous national viewing.

1997 and 2022

Royal funerals

The funerals of Princess Diana and later Queen Elizabeth II functioned as Coronation-type media events, drawing global live audiences around solemn ceremony rather than any competitive outcome.

Where the idea still shows up

What happens to shared ritual when broadcast fragments into streams

Dayan and Katz wrote for an era of a handful of national broadcast channels, when a genuinely shared, simultaneous national audience was still possible. Streaming and on-demand viewing have made that kind of shared, live audience rarer, though not extinct.

Some contemporary events, major sports finals, unexpected national crises, still generate something close to the old media event effect, while platforms have also created new, more fragmented versions built around a shared hashtag or livestream rather than a single broadcast signal.

Why this matters for media literacy
  • It gives journalism a vocabulary for distinguishing ceremonial, agreed-upon coverage from ordinary adversarial reporting
  • Its Contest, Conquest, Coronation typology remains a standard classroom tool for analyzing live broadcast events
  • Its central puzzle, whether shared national ritual survives media fragmentation, is directly picked up by later work on the network society and platform society

Dayan and Katz's own framing. The broadcaster and the event's organizers agree, implicitly, to honor the occasion together rather than report on it the way ordinary news would.

Where the theory ran into trouble

The pushback

The pushback

Critics have questioned how genuinely unifying these events ever were, pointing out that a shared broadcast does not guarantee a shared interpretation, and that different audiences frequently read the same ceremonial moment in sharply different ways.

The framework's original three-channel broadcast environment has largely disappeared, and some scholars argue the concept needs substantial revision, rather than simple extension, to describe fragmented, on-demand, and livestreamed viewing.

Others have countered that platforms have simply produced new kinds of media events, viral livestreams, globally trending crises, built around simultaneous shared attention rather than a single broadcast signal, keeping Dayan and Katz's core insight intact even as its technology changed.

1970s Audience and Reception

Uses and Gratifications

Elihu Katz, Jay Blumler, and Michael Gurevitch Communication researchers
Historical context

Flipping the question from what media does to what audiences want

Almost every theory covered so far in this atlas starts from a version of the same question: what does media do to its audience. Uses and gratifications theory flips that question around entirely, asking instead what audiences do with media, treating them as active, goal-directed users selecting specific content to satisfy specific needs, rather than passive targets absorbing whatever a broadcast happens to send their way.

The approach has a genuinely early precursor. In 1944, the researcher Herta Herzog studied why housewives were so devoted to daytime radio soap operas, and found the answer was not simple distraction. Listeners used the serials for emotional release, for a sense of companionship, and even for practical advice they applied to real problems in their own lives, using the fictional content as a genuine psychological and social tool rather than passively absorbing a story.

Elihu Katz, the same researcher behind Two-Step Flow and Personal Influence, picked the thread back up decades later. Working with Jay Blumler and Michael Gurevitch, Katz helped systematize the approach into a full framework in the early 1970s, most influentially through the 1974 edited volume The Uses of Mass Communications. Their surveys asked audiences directly why they used different media, newspapers, radio, television, and sorted the answers into a set of recurring categories of need.

The shift mattered because it recast the audience's role entirely. Instead of asking how vulnerable a viewer was to a message, uses and gratifications research asked what that viewer was actively looking for, treating media selection itself as a meaningful, purposeful choice rather than a passive event that simply happened to someone.

What fed this theory
  • Herta Herzog's 1944 study of daytime radio soap opera listeners
  • Katz, Blumler, and Gurevitch's 1974 volume, The Uses of Mass Communications
  • Elihu Katz's earlier work on opinion leadership, carried forward into a new framework
  • Growing dissatisfaction with effects-only models that left the audience's own motivations unexamined
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

The active audience

Audiences are treated as goal-directed users who select specific media content to satisfy specific needs, rather than passive recipients simply absorbing whatever they are shown.

Concept 02

Gratification categories

Media use commonly satisfies four broad kinds of need: information and surveillance, personal identity, social integration and interaction, and entertainment or diversion.

Concept 03

Functional alternatives

The same underlying need can be satisfied by different media, or even by activities outside media entirely, meaning the audience's need drives the choice of medium rather than the other way around.

Examples in practice

Where this pattern turned up

United States, 1944

Herzog's radio soap opera study

Listeners described using the serials for emotional release and companionship, and some reported applying advice from the plots to real problems in their own households, decades before the gratifications framework was formally named.

Katz, Blumler, and Gurevitch, 1974

Systematic survey research across media

Their research directly asked audiences why they used newspapers, radio, and television differently, mapping specific, recurring needs onto specific media choices for the first time in a fully systematic way.

News consumption research

Surveillance versus diversion

Studies distinguishing why people follow the news, wanting to stay genuinely informed versus simply wanting a break or a shared topic of conversation, show the identical content serving very different needs for different individuals.

Contemporary parallel

Why people use social media platforms

Modern research into social media use has repeatedly found the same four gratification categories, information, identity, social connection, entertainment, mapping cleanly onto platforms the theory's original authors never imagined.

In one sentence
People don't just get affected by media, they actively choose specific content to meet specific needs, whether that's information, identity, connection, or simple relaxation.

The theory treats media choice itself as data worth studying, asking not just what a broadcast contains but what an audience was actually looking for when they turned it on in the first place.

Where the theory ran into trouble

People aren't always the best judge of their own motives

The pushback

Critics pointed out that the theory relies heavily on self-reported reasons for media use, and people are not always fully aware of, or entirely honest about, their own motivations. Someone might describe watching the news for information while a large part of the real appeal is comfort or habit, a gap self-report surveys struggle to catch.

The theory has also been criticized for focusing so heavily on individual psychological motivation that it can underplay real structural power: audiences can only actively choose among the options media institutions actually make available, a limitation the political economy tradition covered earlier in this atlas takes far more seriously. The four gratification categories, while useful, can also overlap considerably in practice, with a great deal of real media use satisfying several needs at once rather than sorting neatly into a single box.

Affective Publics

Zizi Papacharissi Communication scholar, University of Illinois at Chicago
Historical context

When a hashtag becomes a shared feeling before it becomes an argument

Habermas's public sphere, covered earlier in this atlas, imagined citizens gathering to debate on the strength of their arguments, and Benkler's networked public sphere, covered just before this chapter, imagined that debate spreading through distributed digital networks. Zizi Papacharissi looked closely at what was actually happening on platforms like Twitter during major political events of the early 2010s and found something that fit awkwardly into either picture.

In her 2015 book Affective Publics: Sentiment, Technology, and Politics, Papacharissi studied hashtag-driven movements including the Arab Spring, Occupy Wall Street, and the protests following Michael Brown's death under the hashtag Ferguson, and found that these online publics formed less through careful, reasoned deliberation and more through a shared, rapidly spreading emotional charge, outrage, solidarity, grief, urgency, circulating through networked sharing.

She proposed the term affective publics to describe these temporary, emotionally bonded collectives, groups that come together quickly around a shared hashtag or unfolding event, connected as much by a shared structure of feeling as by any explicit shared argument. Papacharissi also described a related process she called networked storytelling, where an event gets collectively narrated in real time through many individual posts weaving together into one larger, emotionally textured story, rather than being reported by a single centralized account.

Importantly, Papacharissi did not present this as simply a decline from Habermas's more rational ideal. She argued it represented a genuinely different, newly possible mode of political engagement, suited to the particular temporal rhythms of networked media, rapid, real-time, emotionally charged, capable of mobilizing enormous attention very quickly, whatever its limits compared to older, slower forms of deliberation.

What fed this theory
  • Papacharissi's 2015 book, Affective Publics: Sentiment, Technology, and Politics
  • Close study of hashtag activity during the Arab Spring, Occupy Wall Street, and Ferguson protests
  • Earlier public sphere theory, both Habermas's original account and Benkler's networked update
  • Growing scholarly interest in emotion and affect as organizing forces in political life
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

Affective publics

Temporary, emotionally bonded collectives that form around a shared hashtag or event, connected by a shared emotional charge more than by careful, explicit rational deliberation.

Concept 02

Structures of feeling

A shared, circulating emotional tone, rather than a shared argument, that organizes and sustains a networked public's sense of connection and common purpose.

Concept 03

Networked storytelling

An event unfolds and gets collectively narrated in real time through many individual posts weaving together into a larger, shared, emotionally textured story, rather than through one centralized account.

Examples in practice

Where this pattern turned up

2010 to 2011

The Arab Spring

Papacharissi's central case study of hashtag-driven political mobilization, examining how affectively charged, rapidly shared posts helped organize and sustain protest movements across the region.

2011

Occupy Wall Street

A shared emotional charge around economic inequality organized a loosely structured, hashtag-connected protest movement without any single centralized leadership, another of the book's major case studies.

2014

Ferguson

Papacharissi analyzed how the hashtag became a real-time, collectively narrated site of grief, outrage, and political mobilization following Michael Brown's death, a clear instance of an affective public forming rapidly around a breaking event.

Contemporary parallel

Ongoing viral moments of collective emotion

Hashtag-driven movements and viral moments of shared celebration, outrage, or grief continue to demonstrate the same affective public dynamics Papacharissi described, now a routine feature of the contemporary media environment.

In one sentence
On social media, political movements often form around a shared, rapidly spreading emotional charge, connecting people into a temporary public bonded by feeling as much as by argument.

The theory asks a reader to notice the emotional texture of a networked event, not just its stated argument, since that shared feeling may be doing as much of the organizing work as any explicit claim being made.

Where the theory ran into trouble

Feeling mobilizes fast, but doesn't always last

The pushback

Critics have raised real concerns about whether affective, emotionally driven mobilization can sustain itself into the kind of durable organizational structure needed for lasting political change, noting that many hashtag-driven movements achieved enormous, rapid visibility without necessarily translating that attention into concrete institutional or policy outcomes. Others have pointed out that the same rapid-sharing, emotionally charged dynamics that mobilize solidarity and grief can just as easily mobilize misinformation, outrage mobs, or harassment campaigns, since the underlying networked mechanism does not discriminate between prosocial and harmful uses of shared feeling.

Papacharissi's own framing explicitly pushes back on treating this as a simple decline from Habermas's rational-critical ideal, but the tension between emotionally driven mobilization and the kind of sustained, reasoned deliberation earlier public sphere theory valued remains a genuinely open and contested question in the field.

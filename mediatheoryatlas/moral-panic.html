1970s Representation and Power

Moral Panic Theory

Stanley Cohen Sociologist, London School of Economics
Historical context

Two seaside scuffles become a national crisis

Over the Easter weekend of 1964, groups of young people belonging to two loosely defined youth subcultures, the sharply dressed, scooter-riding Mods and the leather-jacketed, motorcycle-riding Rockers, clashed at the English seaside town of Clacton. The actual events were, by most accounts, fairly minor: some scuffles, a bit of vandalism, a handful of arrests, on a cold, wet holiday weekend that had already left a lot of young visitors bored and restless.

The press coverage that followed was anything but minor. Newspapers ran dramatic headlines describing a wave of violence and vandalism sweeping the coast, often inflating damage estimates and using language that painted the Mods and Rockers as a dangerous new breed threatening the country's social fabric. Stanley Cohen, then a young sociologist, spent years studying exactly how that gap opened up between what actually happened at Clacton and what the public came to believe had happened there.

Cohen published his findings in 1972 as Folk Devils and Moral Panics: The Creation of the Mods and Rockers, and in doing so gave the field two lasting terms. He called the demonized groups folk devils, stereotyped symbols onto which a much broader set of social anxieties, about youth, class, and changing postwar Britain, got projected. He called the wider societal reaction a moral panic: an episode in which a condition, group, or person becomes defined as a threat to social values, is presented by media in a stylized and exaggerated way, and provokes a public response often well out of proportion to the actual danger involved.

Cohen also identified what he called a deviancy amplification spiral. Media attention did not just describe the Mods and Rockers conflict, it helped produce more of it, drawing larger and more confrontational crowds to subsequent bank holiday weekends partly because the growing notoriety itself had become part of the appeal, and provoking heavier policing that in turn generated more dramatic headlines.

What fed this theory
  • The 1964 Mods and Rockers disturbances at Clacton and other English seaside towns
  • British tabloid coverage that stylized and amplified the scale of the actual events
  • Cohen's 1972 book, Folk Devils and Moral Panics: The Creation of the Mods and Rockers
  • Broader sociological interest in deviance, labeling, and how social problems get defined
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

Folk devils

Individuals or groups who become symbolic targets, stereotyped and blamed for a much larger, often unrelated set of social anxieties well beyond anything they actually did.

Concept 02

Moral entrepreneurs

The editors, politicians, and community or religious leaders who actively work to define a condition as a genuine threat, mobilizing public concern and often pushing for a formal response.

Concept 03

Deviancy amplification spiral

Media coverage can escalate the very behavior it condemns, both by attracting more people to a now-notorious scene and by provoking a stronger, more visible response from authorities that feeds still more coverage.

Examples in practice

Where this pattern turned up

England, 1964

Mods and Rockers at Clacton

Cohen's founding case study, where a fairly ordinary weekend of youthful disorder was stylized by the press into a symbol of a nation's youth spiraling out of control, complete with harsher sentencing from local courts under public pressure.

United Kingdom, 1980s

The "video nasties" panic

A sustained press and political campaign against violent horror films available on home video helped drive the Video Recordings Act of 1984, a frequently cited case in moral panic research for how closely it followed Cohen's original sequence.

1980s to 1990s

The satanic ritual abuse scares

A wave of media and therapist-driven claims about organized ritual abuse in the United States and United Kingdom led to a number of prosecutions before extensive later investigation found little credible evidence behind most claims, a case sociologists have studied closely as a moral panic that caused genuine, lasting harm.

Contemporary parallel

Recurring youth technology panics

Public alarm over video games, then social media, then specific apps like TikTok has repeatedly followed a strikingly similar press pattern to the original Mods and Rockers coverage, stylized threat, moral entrepreneurs, calls for tighter control.

In one sentence
A minor incident, stylized and exaggerated by media coverage, can become a symbol of social breakdown, and the resulting panic can end up amplifying the very behavior it condemns.

The theory gives media literacy a specific pattern to watch for: a demonized group, inflated language, moral entrepreneurs calling for a crackdown, and a public response that ends up larger than the original problem ever was.

Where the theory ran into trouble

A sharp term, used less carefully over time

The pushback

The phrase moral panic became so widely adopted outside academic research that critics argue its meaning has been diluted, now used loosely in everyday journalism and public debate as a dismissive label for almost any strong public concern, whether or not that concern is actually disproportionate to a real underlying problem. That looseness creates a genuine risk: a legitimate, well-founded worry can get waved away as "just a moral panic" by people who would rather not engage with it seriously.

Cohen's original model was also built around a relatively centralized British press landscape, where a handful of national newspapers could plausibly drive one unified panic. Today's fragmented, algorithmically distributed media environment complicates that picture, since a panic can now emerge simultaneously across many decentralized channels, or fail to cohere into one shared national narrative at all, a dynamic considerably messier than the model Cohen built around 1960s Fleet Street.

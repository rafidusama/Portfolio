2010s Political Economy

Connective Action

W. Lance Bennett and Alexandra Segerberg Political communication researchers
Historical context

A movement without a membership office

Classic social movement research had long assumed a fairly consistent recipe for large-scale collective action: a shared collective identity, a formal organization to coordinate resources, and enough structure to overcome what researchers called the free-rider problem, the risk that individuals benefit from a movement's gains without contributing their own effort. W. Lance Bennett and Alexandra Segerberg looked at a wave of early 2010s digitally organized protests and found this recipe increasingly did not match what they were actually observing.

In their influential 2012 article, later expanded into a 2013 book, both titled The Logic of Connective Action, Bennett and Segerberg proposed a second, genuinely different organizing logic made possible by digital and social media. Rather than adopting one shared organizational message, individuals in a connective action shared their own personalized version of why a given issue mattered to them, often through a hashtag, an image, or a short post, and it was the sheer aggregation of these varied personal expressions, not a single unified campaign, that produced large-scale, visible mobilization.

Crucially, this connective logic required far less formal organizational infrastructure than traditional collective action. Bennett and Segerberg described a spectrum running from organizationally brokered collective action at one end, through hybrid forms, to fully crowd-enabled connective action at the other, where a movement could achieve genuine scale with minimal or even no centralized organizational backbone at all, coordinated instead through the aggregate effect of countless individually shared, personalized expressions.

They studied protests connected to global economic justice and climate mobilizations, including activity surrounding the 2009 Copenhagen climate summit, as early working examples of this looser, more personalized organizational pattern taking shape.

What fed this theory
  • Bennett and Segerberg's 2012 article and 2013 book, both titled The Logic of Connective Action
  • Digitally organized protests around economic justice and climate issues in the early 2010s
  • Classical social movement theory's assumptions about collective identity and formal organization
  • The rise of hashtags and personalized sharing as tools for political expression
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

Collective versus connective logic

Traditional collective action requires a strong shared identity and formal organization. Connective action is organized around personalized, easily shareable expressions of concern, requiring far less formal structure.

Concept 02

Personalized action frames

Each individual expresses their own version of why they care about an issue, rather than adopting one unified organizational message, and it is the aggregation of these varied frames that produces mass mobilization.

Concept 03

The organizational spectrum

A continuum running from organizationally brokered collective action, through hybrid forms, to fully crowd-enabled connective action with minimal formal organizational structure at all.

Examples in practice

Where this pattern turned up

2009, Copenhagen

Climate and economic justice protests

Bennett and Segerberg's own early case studies, loosely coordinated through personalized digital sharing rather than centralized organizing, offered an early working example of connective action logic in practice.

2011

Occupy Wall Street

Also examined by Papacharissi's affective publics theory covered earlier in this atlas, Occupy offers a complementary lens under Bennett and Segerberg's framework, emphasizing its loosely connected, personalized organizational structure alongside its emotional dimension.

2017 onward

The #MeToo movement

Frequently cited in later scholarship extending connective action theory, as millions of individuals shared their own personalized accounts under a shared hashtag, producing massive collective visibility and political pressure with minimal centralized organizational coordination.

Contemporary parallel

Hashtag-organized mutual aid

Rapid, hashtag-organized mutual aid and crowdfunding responses to disasters or crises are frequently cited as further examples of crowd-enabled connective action mobilizing large-scale coordinated response with little formal organizational structure.

The mechanism, demonstrated

Two ways to organize the same movement

Collective action against connective action

Toggle between the traditional organizational logic and the connective logic Bennett and Segerberg described, and notice how differently each structure moves people toward the same broad goal.

In one sentence
Large-scale mobilization no longer necessarily requires a formal organization uniting people around one shared message, it can emerge from thousands of people sharing their own personalized reasons for caring.

The theory asks a reader to notice whether a movement's strength comes from a unified organizational campaign, or from the sheer accumulated weight of countless individually shared, personal expressions of the same underlying concern.

Where the theory ran into trouble

Fast to mobilize, harder to sustain

The pushback

Critics note connective action's loose, personalized structure, while excellent at generating rapid visibility and initial mobilization, can struggle to sustain itself into durable, strategically coordinated political pressure over the long term, since there is often no formal organization left standing to negotiate concrete demands once initial attention fades, a concern that echoes the durability questions raised about Papacharissi's affective publics. Some scholars also note that "personalized" sharing is still shaped and amplified unevenly by platform algorithms and existing influencers, meaning connective action is not as purely bottom-up as the theory's framing might suggest.

Debate also continues over whether collective and connective action logics are truly distinct categories or more often blend together in real movements, with many contemporary campaigns combining loosely networked personalized sharing with at least some degree of formal organizational backbone rather than sitting cleanly at either end of Bennett and Segerberg's spectrum.

Media Dependency Theory

Sandra Ball-Rokeach and Melvin DeFleur Communication researchers
Historical context

Why the same broadcast doesn't always carry the same weight

By the mid-1970s, communication research had produced a genuinely confusing pile of findings. Some studies found media effects were minimal, others found them powerful, and researchers were increasingly aware the honest answer to "how much power does media have" was probably "it depends." Sandra Ball-Rokeach and Melvin DeFleur set out to explain exactly what it depended on.

In a 1976 article, they proposed a systems-level model built around a three-way relationship between the media system, the broader social system, and the audience. Their central claim was that people and societies become more dependent on media for making sense of their world as that world becomes more complex, unstable, or conflict-ridden, and as media comes to serve information functions no other source can easily replace.

Crucially, dependency in their model was not a fixed constant. It was a variable condition that could rise or fall depending on circumstances. During ordinary, stable periods, people rely on media for some things but have other trusted sources, personal experience, family, community, institutions, to check against or fall back on. During periods of genuine social disruption, war, natural disaster, rapid political change, those alternative sources become less available or less reliable, and dependency on media spikes correspondingly.

The theory's most important implication followed directly from that structure: media's potential to actually shape beliefs, feelings, and behavior rises and falls along with dependency itself. The same broadcast can carry dramatically different influence depending on how essential it has become to its audience at that particular moment.

What fed this theory
  • Ball-Rokeach and DeFleur's 1976 article, "A Dependency Model of Mass-Media Effects"
  • Decades of inconsistent findings across the effects tradition, some strong, some weak
  • Systems theory's influence on social science more broadly during this period
  • Real-world cases where media influence visibly spiked during moments of crisis
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

The dependency relationship

A three-way relationship between the media system, the broader social system, and the audience, where reliance on media grows as the surrounding society becomes more complex or unstable.

Concept 02

Structural conditions

Social conflict, rapid change, and crisis increase overall dependency on media, because they weaken or remove the alternative sources people would otherwise use to make sense of an uncertain situation.

Concept 03

Cognitive, affective, and behavioral effects

As dependency deepens, media's potential effects deepen along with it, extending beyond simple beliefs into emotional response and actual behavior, not just what people think but how they feel and act.

Examples in practice

Where this pattern turned up

Natural disasters

Emergency broadcasts and evacuation decisions

During hurricanes, earthquakes, and other emergencies, public dependency on media for critical safety information spikes sharply, and so does media's measurable influence over real behavior like evacuation timing, a frequently cited case in the dependency literature.

Wartime and international crisis

When firsthand information disappears

Ball-Rokeach and DeFleur pointed to periods of social conflict and international crisis as classic conditions for spiking dependency, since ordinary firsthand channels of information become unavailable and audiences turn to media almost entirely to understand what is happening.

Contested elections

Limited personal familiarity with candidates

Voters with little direct, personal knowledge of competing candidates depend heavily on media coverage to form an impression, increasing media's potential influence over their eventual vote compared to more familiar, lower-stakes decisions.

Contemporary parallel

The COVID-19 pandemic

A widely studied recent case where dependency on news and public health broadcasts spiked dramatically during a period of genuine social uncertainty, with corresponding spikes in media's measurable influence on public health beliefs and behavior.

The mechanism, demonstrated

Toggle the social conditions

The same triangle, under different conditions

Switch between a stable society and one in crisis, and watch how the strength of dependency between the audience and the media system changes, even though the underlying three-way structure never does.

In one sentence
The more essential media becomes for making sense of an unstable world, the greater its power to actually shape what people think, feel, and do.

The theory explains why the same broadcast can seem to carry very different weight from one moment to the next. It is not that media power is fixed, it rises and falls with how badly an audience needs it.

Where the theory ran into trouble

A hard variable to pin down precisely

The pushback

The theory is genuinely useful for explaining why media effects research had found such inconsistent results, but critics noted that dependency itself is difficult to measure cleanly as a variable, making the model harder to test with the same precision as more narrowly defined theories like priming or agenda-setting. It is also hard to fully separate rising dependency from the many other things changing simultaneously during a crisis, which complicates any confident claim about cause and effect.

The model was also built around a relatively centralized media system, one where a society could plausibly be said to depend on media as a whole. Today's fragmented, highly personalized media environment complicates that picture, since different audiences may depend on entirely different sources even during the same crisis, weakening the assumption of one shared media system feeding one shared audience.

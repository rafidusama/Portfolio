Datafication

José van Dijck; Nick Couldry & Ulises Mejías Media scholars, developed datafication and data colonialism through the 2010s
Historical context

Your walk to work becomes a data point before you notice it happened

José van Dijck defined datafication as the process of rendering aspects of life that were never previously quantified, a conversation, a friendship, a walk, a facial expression, into data that can be tracked, stored, analyzed, and monetized, a transformation that goes far beyond traditional statistics into the granular texture of everyday behavior.

Where earlier surveillance concerns focused on governments watching citizens, datafication describes something more diffuse: platforms collecting behavioral traces as an ordinary, often invisible byproduct of using a service, a like, a scroll pause, a location ping, none of it necessarily felt as surveillance by the person generating it.

Nick Couldry and Ulises Mejías pushed the argument further with the concept of data colonialism, arguing the scale and structure of contemporary data extraction echoes historical colonialism's logic: a resource, in this case human life itself, appropriated from people who receive little of its resulting value, by a small number of powerful actors who convert it into profit and control.

Both frameworks insist datafication is not a neutral technical process. It requires deciding in advance which parts of life are worth quantifying, embeds particular assumptions about what counts as meaningful behavior, and concentrates the resulting insight and power overwhelmingly in the hands of the platforms doing the collecting.

What fed this theory
  • The mass expansion of smartphone sensors, tracking cookies, and platform behavioral logging through the 2010s
  • Surveillance capitalism's parallel argument about behavioral data as a new kind of raw material
  • Postcolonial theory's framework for analyzing resource extraction and unequal exchange, extended by Couldry and Mejías to data
  • Rising public concern following data scandals, including Cambridge Analytica, over how personal data was collected and used
  • Mediatization theory's account of institutions restructuring themselves around media and, increasingly, data systems
Core concepts

Three ideas doing the work underneath

Concept 01

Datafication

The process of converting previously unquantified aspects of life, movement, mood, relationships, into data points a platform can store, analyze, and monetize, often without the person involved noticing it happening.

Concept 02

Data colonialism

Couldry and Mejías's term for the systemic appropriation of human life as a raw material for profit, echoing historical colonial extraction in its scale, its unequal distribution of benefit, and its normalization as simply how things work.

Concept 03

Data relations

The new, often invisible social relationships datafication creates between ordinary people and the platforms tracking them, relationships marked by extreme information asymmetry: the platform knows a great deal about the person, and the reverse is almost never true.

Examples in practice

Where this idea was put to work

Ongoing

Fitness and health tracking apps

Step counts, heart rates, and sleep patterns, once private bodily experience, become continuously logged data points feeding recommendation systems and, frequently, third-party advertising networks.

2018

The Cambridge Analytica scandal

Millions of Facebook users' data, collected through a seemingly unrelated personality quiz, was repurposed for political targeting, becoming the most widely cited public example of datafication's hidden downstream uses.

Smart city projects

Urban sensor networks

Traffic patterns, foot traffic, and public space usage increasingly get logged by municipal and corporate sensor networks, extending datafication from individual devices to entire physical environments.

Global platforms

Data extraction in the Global South

Couldry and Mejías's data colonialism framework points specifically to how platforms headquartered in wealthy nations extract behavioral data from users worldwide, with the resulting economic value concentrated overwhelmingly in a handful of corporate headquarters.

Where the idea still shows up

The framework behind most contemporary data privacy debates

Nearly every debate about targeted advertising, algorithmic hiring tools, or predictive policing rests on datafication's basic observation: behavior that was never designed to be measured is now measured constantly, and that measurement changes what the behavior means.

Data colonialism has become an increasingly common frame in debates about digital sovereignty in the Global South, where platforms based in wealthy nations extract data and value from users who have little say in how either is governed.

Why this matters for media literacy
  • It gives journalists a vocabulary for reporting on data privacy stories beyond simple breach coverage
  • Its colonial framing connects contemporary platform critique directly to Postcolonial Media Theory elsewhere in this atlas
  • It extends Surveillance Capitalism's argument about behavioral data into a more explicitly global, structural critique

In one sentence
Data colonialism appropriates human life so that data can be continuously extracted from it for profit.

Couldry and Mejías's summary claim. The comparison to historical colonialism is deliberate: a resource taken from people who see little of the resulting value, by a small number of actors who see nearly all of it.

Where the theory ran into trouble

The pushback

The pushback

Critics of the data colonialism framing argue the historical colonialism comparison risks flattening enormously different scales of violence and dispossession into a single metaphor, and that some who study it prefer less loaded terms like data extractivism.

Others note that not all datafication is equally harmful; medical research and public health tracking, for instance, can produce genuine social benefit from exactly the same underlying process critics describe as extraction elsewhere.

Even with these disputes over framing, both concepts share a core, widely accepted claim: the scale and invisibility of contemporary behavioral data collection represent something structurally new, not simply a bigger version of older market research.

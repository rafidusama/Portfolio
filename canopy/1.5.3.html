Module 1 · Ch.5 · 1.5.3

Advanced

This is the interview where the power imbalance runs the other way from the previous two modules. A scientist or official can decline to answer with little cost. Someone who has lost their land, their water, or their livelihood is often speaking to you from a position with far less power, and far more at stake in how their words are used. The craft here is getting real, reportable material without extracting it.

Informed Consent in Practice

Consent isn't a formality you get once at the start. It means the person actually understands how their name, image, and words might be used, who might see them, and what risks that could carry for them locally — and that they're comfortable with all of that before you begin, and still comfortable with it by the time you leave.

Power Imbalance Awareness

You Hold More Power Than It Feels Like

Your notebook, your outlet, and your ability to leave when the interview is over all carry weight the person you're interviewing may not have. Acting on that awareness, not just being aware of it, is the actual skill.

Standing to Speak for Themselves

Let the person frame their own experience where possible, rather than only confirming a narrative you arrived with. If what they say complicates your expected story, that complication is usually worth keeping.

Avoiding Leading Questions

Leading

"It must have been devastating to lose everything — how did that feel?" This supplies the emotion and the framing before the person has said anything themselves.

Open

"What was that day like for you?" This leaves room for whatever the person actually experienced, including responses a reporter might not have anticipated.

Trauma-Informed Basics

Pace and Control Belong to Them

Let the person set the pace, skip questions without justifying why, and stop the interview at any point. Avoid pressing for detail on a painful moment beyond what they volunteer — the goal is accurate, reportable material, not the most dramatic possible quote.

Language and Translation

Working through an interpreter, or across a regional dialect, changes what's possible in an interview. Brief your interpreter beforehand on tone as well as content, and be alert to moments where a translation is smoothing over something the original phrasing may have carried more weight in.

Field Tool

Question Bank — Affected Resident

Open, non-leading questions organized by what they're built to do.

Opening & Consent
  1. Before we start, is it alright if I take notes and possibly use your name? You can also choose not to be named, or to speak generally without personal details.
  2. Would you rather I ask questions, or would you prefer to just tell me what feels important to you first?
Understanding Their Experience
  1. Can you walk me through what a typical day looks like for you now, compared to a few years ago?
  2. What's changed that you notice most in your daily life?
Getting Specific, Reportable Detail
  1. Can you tell me about one specific morning or moment that stands out to you?
  2. Is there something about this you think people outside your community don't understand or often get wrong?
Closing With Care
  1. Is there anything you said that you'd like to say differently, or not include?
  2. Is there anything you wish I had asked, that I didn't?

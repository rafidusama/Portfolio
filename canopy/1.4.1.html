Foundations

The nut graf is the paragraph, usually the second or third, that tells the reader why this story exists: why this, why now, why should they keep reading. Everything above it is a hook. Everything below it has to deliver on what it promised. Get it wrong and even a well-reported story can lose the reader before the real work of the piece begins.

Anatomy of a Nut Graf

A working nut graf usually does three things in quick succession: names the specific thing that happened or was found, states what's actually at stake because of it, and signals what the rest of the story is going to show. It doesn't summarize the whole piece — it earns the reader's decision to keep going.

Common Weak Patterns

The Topic Sentence

"Environmental pollution is a growing concern in Bangladesh." True, but it names a topic, not a story — it could sit atop almost any piece ever written on the subject.

The Buried Stakes

The specific finding is there, but what it means for the reader or the affected community is left implicit, forcing the reader to do the work the writer should have done.

The Over-Promise

States a stake the story doesn't actually deliver on later — "will reveal for the first time" when the story mostly restates known findings.

The Hedge Pile-Up

"Some experts suggest this could potentially be linked to..." — so many qualifiers stack up that the reader can't tell what's actually being claimed.

The Story Contract

Think of the nut graf as a contract you sign with the reader. Whatever you promise there — a specific wrongdoing, a specific human cost, a specific unanswered question — the rest of the story has to deliver on, in the order and proportion the nut graf implied. A reader who feels the contract was broken, even unconsciously, trusts the next story less.

"If your nut graf promises an investigation into who\'s responsible, and paragraph four is still describing the weather, you\'ve already broken the contract."

Module 4 · Ch.2 · 4.2.1

Field Craft

Bangladesh's environmental legal framework is larger and older than most reporters realize — one scholarly count put the number of laws with a direct or indirect bearing on the environment at around 185. You won't need most of them day to day, but knowing the core framework and where to look for the rest changes what you can credibly assess in a story.

The Core Framework

The Bangladesh Environment Conservation Act, 1995 (ECA) is the foundational law — it established the Department of Environment, created the office of the Director General, introduced the Environmental Clearance Certificate system, and gave government the power to declare Ecologically Critical Areas. It's implemented through the Environment Conservation Rules, 1997 (ECR), later updated by the Environment Conservation Rules, 2023. A separate Bangladesh Environment Court Act (originally 2000, amended 2010) was meant to create a dedicated environment court in every district — implementation has lagged well behind that goal.

The Environmental Clearance Process

Industrial units and projects are classified into four categories based on pollution potential and impact: Green, Yellow, Orange, and Red. Green category projects (minimal impact) get faster, simpler clearance; Red category (highest impact) requires the fullest environmental assessment. An Environmental Clearance Certificate for a Green category project is valid for three years; for other categories, one year — meaning renewal, and whether it's actually enforced, is a recurring, checkable story.

Green

Minimal impact — simplified, fast-track clearance.

Yellow

Low-to-moderate impact — standard review.

Orange

Significant impact — fuller assessment required.

Red

Highest impact — full environmental impact assessment required.

A decision can be appealed under Section 14 of the Act — worth checking whenever a clearance decision itself becomes the story.

Where Enforcement Breaks Down

Wide Powers, Uneven Use

The Director General holds substantial enforcement powers — fines, forced closure, even directing utility providers to cut electricity, gas, or water to a noncompliant facility — but must first issue written notice and a response deadline. Legal critics have pointed to vague technical-qualification requirements for enforcement officials as a structural weakness.

The environment court system illustrates the gap between law and practice especially clearly: courts were meant to exist in every district, but rollout has been limited, and procedurally, an affected person generally cannot approach the court directly — a DoE-appointed investigator's report is required first, adding a real bottleneck.

"A law that's nearly impossible to enforce in practice — like restrictions on smoke-emitting vehicles in a city of millions dependent on aging public transport — is still real law. It's just a different kind of story: not 'is this illegal,' but 'why does illegal keep happening anyway.'"

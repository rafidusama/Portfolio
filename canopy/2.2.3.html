Advanced

A carbon credit is a claim that a specific amount of emissions was reduced or avoided because of a specific project. The entire market depends on that claim being true and not double-counted — which means the reporter's job is less about explaining the concept and more about testing whether a specific claim actually holds up.

Compliance vs. Voluntary Markets

Compliance Markets

Government-regulated systems where companies are legally required to hold credits or allowances up to a set emissions cap — generally more tightly verified.

Voluntary Markets

Companies or individuals buy credits voluntarily, often for public sustainability claims — verification standards vary widely and enforcement is generally weaker.

Most Bangladesh-based carbon projects that make the news — reforestation, mangrove restoration, cookstove programs — sell into voluntary markets, which is exactly why extra scrutiny matters: there's no regulator checking the claim before it's sold.

Common Integrity Problems

Double Counting and Phantom Credits

Double counting happens when the same emissions reduction is claimed by more than one party — for instance, both a host country's national climate target and a company's voluntary offset purchase. Phantom credits are issued for reductions that would have happened anyway, or that never materialized as claimed — a project that doesn't clearly establish "additionality" (proof the reduction wouldn't have happened without it) is at high risk of this.

Evaluating a Bangladesh-Based Claim

A credible project should be able to name its verification standard, show credits actually issued on a public registry (not just planned or pledged), demonstrate additionality, and show ongoing — not one-time — monitoring. A project that can't answer these clearly is a project whose claim you shouldn't report uncritically.

"A tree-planting announcement is not a verified carbon credit. Ask to see the registry listing, not the press release."

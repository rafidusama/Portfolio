Field Craft

A chart makes a claim, the same way a sentence does. Choosing the wrong chart type for the data doesn't just look wrong — it can misrepresent what the data actually shows, sometimes more seriously than a misworded sentence would.

Matching Chart Type to Data Type

Line

Change over time — a trend, trajectory, or trend reversal.

Bar

Comparison across discrete categories or places at one point in time.

Map

Geographic distribution — where something is concentrated or spread.

Scatter

Relationship between two variables — whether they move together.

Before/After

A dramatic visual contrast at the same location across time.

Common Distortions

Truncated Axis

A line or bar chart with a y-axis that doesn't start at zero can make a small change look dramatic — a shift from 40% to 42% can look like a doubling if the axis only spans 39–43%.

Full Axis

Starting the axis at zero (or clearly disclosing why not) lets the visual size of the change match its actual proportional size.

Misleading Map Bins

Poorly chosen color breakpoints on a choropleth map can make a mild regional difference look stark, or hide a real disparity inside one broad color band.

Disclosed, Sensible Bins

Choosing breakpoints that reflect meaningful thresholds in the data — and disclosing whether the map shows raw counts or per-capita rates — keeps the map honest.

Pairing Visual With Prose

The chart should carry the pattern; the prose should carry the meaning. Repeating every number from the chart in the surrounding paragraph makes both feel redundant — instead, use the prose to explain why the pattern matters, what caused it, or what it means for the reader, while letting the visual do the work of showing it.

"If your caption just restates the chart's numbers, the chart didn't need a caption — it needed a different chart, or the paragraph needed a different job."

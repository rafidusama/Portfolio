Field Craft

A scientist can give you the most accurate material in your entire story, or the most easily misquoted. The difference usually comes down to preparation: whether you arrive with technical questions ready, whether you ask for a plain-language version before you leave, and whether you check your own understanding against theirs before you file.

Preparing Technical Questions in Advance

Read enough beforehand to ask a specific question, not a general one. "Tell me about the study" invites a lecture. "Your abstract mentions a confidence interval of X to Y — what does that range actually mean for someone living in the affected area?" invites a usable answer.

Ask for the Plain Version

Explicitly ask: "If you had to explain this to someone with no science background, in one sentence, what would you say?" Scientists are used to writing for peers, not readers — asking directly often gets you the clearest quote of the whole interview.

Separate Measured from Modeled

Ask explicitly what was directly measured versus what was projected or modeled. Conflating the two is one of the most common ways science reporting overstates certainty.

Fact-Checking Without Ceding Control

It's good practice to read a technical quote back to a source to confirm you've represented it accurately — this is different from letting them approve your story or rewrite your framing. Be specific about what you're checking: "I want to make sure I've got this number and its meaning right" is a fact-check. "Does this paragraph work for you?" is an editorial handover you should avoid.

When a Scientist Won't Commit to a Plain Answer

The Hedging Expert

Some scientists, especially on contested topics, will only speak in heavily qualified academic language, wary of being misquoted or oversimplified. Don't force a false certainty into their mouth. Instead, ask them to state the range and what it means in practice, and use their hedge as part of the story's honest description of uncertainty — rather than dropping the quote or paraphrasing away their caution.

"Their caution is data too. Report it as carefully as you'd report their number."

Foundations

Most environmental stories don't need a data scientist — they need a reporter who knows which of a handful of major databases already has the number they're looking for, and how to tell a genuinely raw measurement from someone else's interpretation of one.

This module covers major international and global datasets. For Bangladesh-specific official portals, see Pillar 4, Module 3.1 (Bangladesh-Specific Datasets & Official Sources).

Three Categories of Data Sources

Government Portals

Official national and international bodies publish primary data, though openness and update frequency vary widely — see Module 4.3.1 for Bangladesh specifics.

International Databases

Cross-country platforms like EM-DAT and OpenAQ aggregate and standardize data across many nations, making comparison possible.

NGO & Research Datasets

Organizations like the World Resources Institute (behind Global Forest Watch) or CRED (behind EM-DAT) combine satellite feeds, field data, and modeling into usable public platforms.

Raw Data vs. Processed Data

Raw Data

A single sensor's minute-by-minute reading, an unprocessed satellite image, a direct government record — closer to the original measurement, but often requires you to do your own analysis.

Processed / Interpreted Data

An aggregated index, a risk score, a modeled projection — genuinely useful, but it already carries someone else's analytical choices. Know whose interpretation you're citing.

Neither is inherently better — a processed dataset like an AQI reading is more usable on deadline than raw sensor output, but citing it as if it were a direct measurement understates the interpretation baked into it.

A concrete example: NASA FIRMS detects heat signatures via satellite — genuinely raw sensor data. Global Forest Watch's fire alerts layer takes that same raw data and processes it into a usable near-real-time alert system. Citing "GFW says there's a fire here" and "NASA's raw satellite data shows a heat signature here" are both defensible, but they're not quite the same claim.

"Know which link in the chain you're actually citing — the sensor, or someone's interpretation of the sensor."

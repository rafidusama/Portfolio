Field Craft

The same tip can become three completely different stories, and picking the wrong one wastes the tip. A rumor about a factory's effluent could become a same-day explainer of what the regulation actually requires, a feature on the fishing community living downstream, or a weeks-long investigation into who approved the permit and why. Choosing which one to pursue is its own skill, separate from the reporting itself.

Decision Criteria

Evidence in Hand

A tip alone supports an explainer. A document or two supports a feature. A substantial, checkable body of evidence supports an investigation.

Time Available

Same-day pressure favors an explainer using public information. Open-ended time supports the deeper reporting an investigation needs.

The Core Question

"What is this and why does it matter" points toward an explainer or feature. "Who did this and are they hiding it" points toward an investigation.

Matching Format to What You Actually Have

A common mistake is picking the format you want to write rather than the one the evidence and time actually support. Starting an investigation with only a tip and a deadline tomorrow usually produces a thin, unconvincing piece — the same tip filed as a same-day explainer, with the investigation pursued properly over the following weeks, usually serves the reader and the story better.

One Tip, Three Stories

A resident calls to say a factory is dumping waste into a canal. Filed today with public regulation and one on-record quote, it's an explainer on what the rules require and whether this looks like a violation. Filed over a few days with a visit to the site and interviews with affected families, it's a feature on what living next to that canal is actually like. Pursued over weeks with permit records, effluent testing, and a paper trail on approvals, it's an investigation into how the violation was allowed to continue.

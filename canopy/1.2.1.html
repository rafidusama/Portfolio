Foundations

You don't need a science degree to report on science responsibly. You need to know what a study can and can't tell you, and enough structure to tell a solid finding from a shaky one — before it becomes your headline.

Anatomy of a Scientific Paper

Abstract

The short summary. Useful for orientation, but never the whole story — it's written to sell the finding, not to qualify it.

Methods

How the data was collected. A small or narrow sample here should soften how confidently you report the finding.

Findings

What was actually measured. Distinguish what the data shows from what the authors speculate it might mean.

Limitations

Often skipped by readers, and by headlines. This is where the authors tell you where not to over-extend their own finding.

A Source Credibility Ladder

Peer-Reviewed Study

Checked by independent experts before publication. Not infallible, but the strongest baseline for a factual claim.

Preprint

Shared before peer review. Can be legitimate and timely, but treat findings as provisional and say so in your copy.

Press Release

Written to be picked up, not to be precise. Trace claims back to the underlying study before repeating them.

Advocacy Report

May contain real data, but is produced to support a position. Useful as a lead, not as a stand-alone citation.

Confidence Is Not Certainty

A "statistically significant" result means a pattern is unlikely to be due to random chance — it does not mean the effect is large, important, or certain. A wide confidence interval means the true value could sit anywhere in a broad range. Reporting a number without its uncertainty is often more misleading than not reporting it at all.

Where Bangladeshi coverage most often loses precision: a hedged, narrow scientific finding gets flattened into a confident, general headline somewhere between the study and the front page. Your job on this beat is to be the point where that flattening stops.

Field Craft

"Scientists are unsure" is one of the most common headlines in environmental reporting, and one of the most misleading. It usually means something much more specific: unsure about the exact magnitude, unsure about attributing a single event, unsure about the timeline. Collapsing all of that into "unsure" tells the reader almost nothing, and it often does more damage than a wrong number would — because it invites the reader to distrust the science itself rather than understand its actual shape.

The job on this beat is not to remove uncertainty from a story. Real uncertainty is part of how honest science works. The job is to describe that uncertainty precisely enough that the reader ends up better calibrated than before they read it, not worse.

Framing Ranges and Probabilities in Prose

Most climate and risk findings come as a range or a probability, not a single number. "Salinity could rise between 20% and 45% by 2050" is a very different claim from "salinity will rise 45% by 2050," even though the second is technically inside the range of the first. Reporting only the upper bound isn't lying, exactly — it's cherry-picking the most dramatic true thing instead of the most representative true thing.

A workable habit: if the source gives you a range, put the range in the story, not just one end of it. If the source gives you a probability, say what it's a probability of — "a 1-in-20 chance in any given year" reads very differently from "unlikely," even though a forecaster might use both to describe the same number.

Real Uncertainty vs. Manufactured Doubt

Scientific Uncertainty

A genuine, quantifiable range in a model or measurement — for example, disagreement between climate models on exactly how much a river's flow will change, even though all models agree on the direction of change.

Manufactured Doubt

A disputed claim from a party with a financial or political interest in the outcome, dressed up to look like the same kind of uncertainty — for example, a polluter disputing well-established measurement data with no competing evidence of their own.

Treating both kinds of "disagreement" as equally weighted is the false-balance trap. The test is simple: does the disputing party have independent evidence, or only an interest in the answer?

Case Examples: Forecasting Under Uncertainty

Flood Risk

A flood forecast is usually expressed as a probability of a river crossing a danger level within a time window, not a certainty. Reporting "the river will flood this week" when the actual forecast is "a significant chance of crossing the danger mark within five days" overstates certainty in a way that can backfire — if the flood arrives a few days late, the story reads as having been wrong, when the forecast itself was accurate.

Cyclone Intensity

Cyclone tracks and intensities are forecast as a cone of probability, not a single line. Two forecasters using slightly different intensity ranges for the same storm are not "in dispute" — they're describing the same underlying uncertainty with different but compatible numbers. Framing that as forecasters being "split" invents a disagreement that isn't really there.

Field Craft

Most disaster coverage lives in the first 72 hours and then disappears. The more valuable and harder-to-find stories are usually in the months after, when reconstruction promises are tested against what actually gets built.

The Disaster Reporting Arc

Warning

Forecast issued, evacuation orders, preparedness gaps.

Impact

Immediate damage, casualties, first response.

Response

Relief distribution, shelter, early coordination.

Recovery

Rebuilding, livelihoods, promised aid delivery.

Accountability

Were promises kept? What changed structurally?

Early-Warning Systems and BMD Forecasting

The Bangladesh Meteorological Department issues staged cyclone warning signals — knowing the current signal number tells you the expected severity and how much lead time communities have had. A story on whether warnings actually reached the most exposed communities in time is often more revealing than the storm itself.

Where Recovery Stories Get Dropped

Most newsroom attention leaves within the first two weeks, exactly when displaced households are settling into what often becomes months of limbo. Planning a follow-up story deliberately, rather than waiting for another news hook, is usually the only way it gets covered at all.

Cyclone Mocha — Coordinated Relief Coverage

Cyclone Mocha's 2023 landfall near the Bangladesh-Myanmar border prompted coordinated relief coverage involving government agencies, international humanitarian organizations, and local reporters — a useful reference case for how warning, evacuation, and early response can be covered together as a connected sequence rather than isolated news items.

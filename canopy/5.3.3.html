Field Craft

Environmental journalism runs disproportionately on grant funding, NGO partnerships, and sponsored access — not because reporters are less rigorous, but because the beat is chronically under-resourced. That makes disclosure practice a core professional skill here, not an occasional formality.

NGO and Donor-Funded Reporting

Funding Doesn't Disqualify the Work

Grant-funded environmental journalism is common and can be entirely independent — the funding relationship itself isn't the problem if editorial control stays with the newsroom.

Disclosure Is the Safeguard

Telling the reader plainly who funded the reporting, and confirming the funder had no editorial input, is what lets the reader trust the work despite the funding relationship.

Corporate Access Trade-Offs

A sponsored site visit or an exclusive interview arranged by a company's PR team comes with an implicit expectation, whether or not it's ever stated outright. Disclosing the arrangement, and being honest with yourself about whether it shaped what you were able to see and ask, protects both your credibility and the story's accuracy.

Independence in a Resource-Constrained Newsroom

When a grant or sponsored trip is the only realistic way to report a story at all, the answer isn't to avoid the opportunity — it's to be transparent about it and to actively guard against the arrangement shaping your findings. Readers can accept a funded story; they can't accept a hidden one.

"Disclosure doesn't apologize for the arrangement. It's what makes the arrangement acceptable in the first place."

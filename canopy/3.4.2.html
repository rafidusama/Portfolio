Advanced

An Environmental Impact Assessment is written by the project's own consultants, submitted to secure approval. That doesn't make it worthless — it makes it a document you read the way you'd read any interested party's account: carefully, and against independent verification.

EIA Structure and Required Disclosures

Project Description

What's being built, where, and at what scale.

Baseline Conditions

The environment's condition before the project, against which impact is measured.

Impact Prediction

Projected effects on air, water, land, and communities.

Mitigation Measures

What the project proposes to do to reduce predicted harm.

Alternative Sites

Whether other locations or designs were seriously considered.

Public Consultation

Record of community input gathered during the assessment process.

Spotting Omissions or Understated Impacts

Watch for Assertion Without Evidence

A conclusion like "no significant impact expected" is only as strong as the analysis behind it. If a claim isn't backed by a specific methodology or data in the same section, that's worth flagging — and worth checking against an independent source.

Comparing EIA Claims Against Independent Data

The EIA's own conclusions are one input, not the final word. An independent academic study, a civil society technical review, or your own satellite comparison (see Module 3.2.2) can confirm or contradict a specific claim — and where they diverge, that divergence is often the actual story.

"An EIA answering every question on paper isn't the same as answering them rigorously. Read for evidence, not just coverage."

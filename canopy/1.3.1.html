Field Craft

A statistic tells a reader that something is true. A scene lets them feel why it matters. "Groundwater levels have dropped 12% in five years" is accurate and almost immediately forgettable. A woman walking forty minutes further to the nearest working tube well than she did five years ago, told in one concrete moment, is the same fact — and it's the one the reader remembers.

Finding the Person Behind the Number

Every aggregate statistic is made of individual cases. Your job is to find one that's representative, not extreme — a household experiencing the median version of the problem, not the single worst case you could locate. An extreme outlier makes for vivid writing but can mislead the reader about how widespread or severe the issue actually is.

"Look for someone whose ordinary week has been reshaped by the number, not someone whose life has been destroyed by it — unless the statistic is actually about destruction."

Scene-Building Basics

Three Elements a Working Scene Needs

  • A specific sensory detail. Not "the water was dirty" but what it looked like, smelled like, felt like against skin.
  • A specific action, not a general description. Not "she struggles to find water" but "she filled the same clay pot her mother used, twice, before either tap ran clear."
  • A moment, not a summary of a lifetime. Anchor the scene in one specific morning, one specific walk, one specific harvest — not "every day for years."

Avoiding Reductive Victim Framing

The "Poverty Porn" Trap

A scene that reduces a person to their suffering — describing them only through loss, deprivation, and hardship, with no trace of agency, competence, or ordinary personhood — flattens them into a symbol rather than presenting them as someone the reader could recognize. The fix isn't to soften the hardship. It's to include at least one detail that shows the person acting, deciding, or adapting, not just enduring.

Field Craft

Solutions journalism is not good news. It's rigorous reporting on a response to a documented problem, held to the same evidentiary standard as an investigation into what's broken. The distinction matters because "good news" stories tend to skip the parts that make solutions journalism useful: the evidence that something actually worked, and the honesty about what it didn't.

What Makes It Solutions Journalism

Responds to a Real Problem

The story names the specific, documented problem the response addresses — not just "things are bad," but the precise failure being responded to.

Shows Evidence of Impact

Not intentions or announcements — actual measured or observed effect, even if modest or partial.

Discloses Limitations

States plainly what the response hasn't solved, who it hasn't reached, or where it might fail — this is what separates journalism from a press release.

Offers Transferable Insight

Explains enough about how the response works that another community or reporter could learn something applicable elsewhere.

Floating Gardens as Climate Adaptation

Floating gardens — a traditional technique adapted for waterlogged and flood-prone areas — are often cited as a solutions story in Bangladeshi climate coverage. Told well, the story explains the specific waterlogging problem it responds to, shows evidence of crop yield or livelihood impact where it's been adopted, and is honest that it isn't practical everywhere or for every crop. Told poorly, it becomes a feel-good feature with no evidence beyond a few photographs.

Avoiding "Solutions-Washing"

When a Government or Company Hands You the Story

Be especially careful when a solutions story idea arrives via a government press release or corporate sustainability announcement. The interested party has a reason to want the "solution" framing reported uncritically. Apply the same evidence standard you'd apply to any other claim from an interested source — independently verify the impact, don't just report the announcement.

A story that only praises an intervention, without naming the problem's scale relative to the response, functions as advocacy for that intervention, whether or not that was the intent.

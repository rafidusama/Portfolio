Field Craft

A single character can carry a story's emotional weight, but the story still has to be about the system, not just the person. The craft here is holding both at once: a reader should finish the piece remembering a specific human being, and understanding a structural problem that extends well beyond them.

Choosing a Character Responsibly

The right central character is representative, not extreme. Someone experiencing the median version of the problem teaches the reader more about how widespread it is than someone experiencing the worst possible version — even though the worst case might make for a more dramatic scene. It also matters whether the person understands and consents to how their story will be used, and whether they have the standing to speak for themselves rather than being spoken for entirely by the reporter's framing.

Balancing Individual Narrative and Structure

When a Story Needs a Character

When the point is to make an abstract, systemic pattern legible and memorable — a policy failure, a slow-moving crisis, a statistic too large to feel real on its own.

When It Doesn't

When the story is fundamentally about a mechanism, a document, or a dataset, and a character would decorate the piece without adding understanding — for example, a straightforward explainer of how a policy works.

A long-form feature will usually need both modes and has to move between them deliberately, which is what the teardown below is built to show.

The One-Character Trap

Relying on a single character for an entire long feature risks implying their specific experience is universal. A second or third character, even briefly, shows the reader the range of ways the same systemic problem plays out.

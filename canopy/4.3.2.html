Foundations

Bangladesh's environmental stories are built on a small set of physical systems that repeat across almost every beat in this toolkit — the same rivers, the same delta, the same mangrove forest. Knowing the basic shape of these systems is what lets you recognize when a story fits a known pattern versus something genuinely new.

River Systems & Hydrology

Bangladesh's rivers form four major networks: the Brahmaputra-Jamuna system (originating in Tibet, entering Bangladesh from India), the Ganges-Padma system, the Surma-Meghna system, and the Chittagong region rivers (including the Karnaphuli, dammed at Kaptai for hydroelectric power). Together these carry an estimated 2.4 billion tonnes of sediment to the Bay of Bengal each year — the same silt that makes the delta fertile also makes it prone to catastrophic flooding. The rivers are simultaneously the country's greatest resource and its greatest hazard.

Wetland Types

Beels

Shallow lakes in floodplain depressions, common across the delta.

Baors

Oxbow lakes formed when a river bend gets cut off from the main channel.

Haors

Deeply flooded depressions concentrated in the northeast, supporting seasonal wetland ecosystems and diverse bird life.

The Sundarbans Ecosystem

The Sundarbans is the world's largest single tract of mangrove forest, spanning roughly 10,000 km² across Bangladesh and India, with the Bangladesh portion covering 6,017 km² — about 4.13% of the country's total land area and 38.12% of its total forest land. It contains an estimated 50% of the world's mangrove plant species and roughly 505 wildlife species, including 261 to 320 bird species.

Ecologically, the forest divides into three zones based on salinity — freshwater, moderate saltwater, and saltwater — each dominated by different tree species: sundri (the forest's namesake tree) in less saline zones, with gewa and goran becoming more dominant toward the saltier zones. Understanding which zone a specific reporting location sits in explains a lot about what species and pressures you'll actually encounter there.

Agro-Ecological Zones

Bangladesh is officially divided into 30 agro-ecological zones (AEZs) — a classification combining land type, soil, flooding depth, and growing season used in agricultural and environmental planning. A coastal saline AEZ and a hill-tract AEZ face almost entirely different vulnerabilities even within the same country, which is why "Bangladeshi agriculture" as a single category rarely captures the actual story.

Beyond the Delta: Other Key Systems

Chittagong Hill Tracts

Hilly terrain hosting tropical evergreen and semi-evergreen forest, over 2,000 flowering plant species, and indigenous communities with distinct land-use practices.

Coastal & Marine Zone

An offshore marine ecosystem supporting around 169 species of brackish-water and marine fish, roughly 65% of them edible — a significant, under-covered fisheries and marine-health beat.

Field Craft

Balance is a real journalistic virtue, which is exactly what makes false balance so easy to fall into. The instinct to present "both sides" feels like fairness. But when one side is backed by a body of independent evidence and the other is an interested party's denial, treating them as equally weighted isn't balance — it's a distortion dressed up as neutrality.

The False-Balance Trap in Climate Coverage

Climate coverage is especially prone to this because the underlying science is often settled while the politics around it are not. A story can quote a scientist and a skeptical official back to back and feel scrupulously fair, while actually leaving the reader with the impression that credible experts are split roughly down the middle — when the literature shows nothing of the kind.

"Fairness means giving weight in proportion to the evidence, not giving every voice in the room equal space."

Two Kinds of Questions

A Consensus Question

Has a factual answer that evidence can settle: did this factory's effluent cause the fish kill? Is the region's rainfall pattern shifting? These deserve weight proportional to the evidence, not equal-time treatment.

A Policy Question

Involves genuine value trade-offs that evidence alone can't resolve: is a costly water-treatment mandate worth the economic cost to small businesses? These genuinely warrant multiple viewpoints presented fairly.

The most common false-balance mistake is applying policy-question fairness to a consensus question — treating "did this happen" with the same even-handedness that "what should we do about it" actually deserves.

Handling Politically Sensitive Claims

When an Official Contradicts the Data

You still have to report what they said — it's part of the record, especially if they hold power over the issue. The skill is in placement and context: putting the contradicted claim next to the evidence it contradicts, in the same breath, rather than letting it stand alone as if it were equally credible. Omitting the quote entirely can look like you're taking a side; running it unchallenged can look like you're endorsing it. The middle path is naming both clearly.

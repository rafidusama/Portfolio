Advanced

A satellite image comparison can show what an official denial can't — whether a riverbank has actually eroded, whether a factory has actually expanded, whether a forest has actually shrunk. It's often the only evidence available for places you can't safely or practically visit.

Free vs. Paid Tiers

Free: Copernicus & NASA

The Copernicus Browser (successor to the now-deprecated EO Browser) gives free access to the full Sentinel satellite archive, with built-in comparison and timelapse tools. NASA Worldview offers free near-real-time and historical imagery.

Paid: Higher Resolution

Planet's commercial imagery offers much higher resolution (down to well under a meter per pixel) than free public satellites, useful when Sentinel's resolution isn't fine enough to see the specific detail your story needs — but it requires a paid subscription for full access.

Building a Before/After Comparison

The comparison is only as credible as its consistency: match the season, ideally the same month in different years, to avoid a wet-season image being compared against a dry-season one and mistaking normal seasonal variation for genuine change. Both the Copernicus Browser and NASA Worldview support side-by-side or split-screen comparison directly in the tool.

Use Cases

River encroachment — compare a riverbank's edge across years to document informal construction or erosion. Flooding extent — compare imagery just before and during a flood event to map affected area. Industrial expansion — track a facility's footprint growing over time, useful when the company itself won't confirm its scale.

Imagery as Evidence Where You Can't Go

For sites with restricted or denied access — a closed industrial facility, a politically sensitive border area, a hard-to-reach forest interior — satellite imagery may be the only way to independently verify a claim. It doesn't replace on-the-ground reporting, but it can confirm or contradict an official denial when nothing else can.

"A company can refuse your call. It can't stop the satellite from passing overhead."

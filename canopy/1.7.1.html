Field Craft

A photograph or a shot of video can carry more weight than any paragraph in the story around it, which means it carries proportionally more responsibility. The same flood can be shown in a frame that helps a reader understand its scale, or a frame that only shocks. The same interview can be filmed in a way that respects the person speaking, or one that reduces them to their worst day. This module covers the choices that separate the two.

Framing Choices That Convey Scale

Environmental harm is often diffuse — a river degraded over a decade, a coastline eroding by meters a year. A single frame has to do work that a paragraph would do with numbers and comparisons. The tools for this are consistent across photography and video.

Human Scale Reference

Including a person or familiar object in frame gives the viewer an instant, intuitive sense of size — a pile of waste is abstract; a pile of waste taller than the person standing next to it is immediate.

Wide Establishing Shot

Pulling back to show surrounding context — how far the damage extends, what sits next to it — tells the systemic story a close-up can't.

Aerial or Elevated Perspective

A drone or elevated shot can reveal a pattern invisible from the ground — the true extent of deforestation or river encroachment often only reads from above.

Before/After Framing

Matching the same location, angle, and time of day across two dates lets the image carry the comparison a paragraph would otherwise need to argue for.

Avoiding Disaster-Porn Imagery

There's a real difference between a photograph that helps a reader understand suffering and one that displays it for impact. The test isn't whether the image is difficult to look at — some true images are. It's whether the framing serves understanding or spectacle.

Spectacle

A tight, close-up shot of a person's visible distress, isolated from any context about cause, response, or what happens next — the image exists only to provoke a reaction.

Understanding

A shot that includes the person alongside the specific cause of their situation — a damaged embankment, a dry well — giving the viewer the same information a caption would otherwise have to supply alone.

Captioning Standards

Who, What, When, Where

A complete caption identifies the subject, the action or condition shown, the date, and the specific location — vague captions ("a flooded area in Bangladesh") do the reader and the subject a disservice.

Hedge Unverified Claims

If a caption asserts a cause you haven't independently confirmed ("flooding caused by upstream construction"), hedge it ("residents say is linked to") until it's verified.

Consent for Photographing Affected People

The same consent principles from interviewing apply to being photographed, arguably with higher stakes — a face is far more identifiable and permanent than a quote. Explain clearly how the image might be used and where it might appear, and if a subject is in a vulnerable position (children, people in an unstable legal situation, people facing social stigma for the situation being photographed), consider whether an image that doesn't show their face, or a wider shot that doesn't isolate them individually, can still tell the story.

When in Doubt, Widen the Shot

A wide shot showing a community or a scene, rather than a tight portrait of one identifiable person, often carries nearly the same informational value with substantially less risk to any individual within it.

Video-Specific Considerations

B-Roll Ethics

Cutaway and establishing footage should represent the actual scene, not be swapped in from an unrelated time or place to "improve" the visuals — misrepresenting b-roll as documentary footage of the specific event is a fabrication risk unique to video.

Interview Framing on Video

Camera angle and eye-line affect how trustworthy or vulnerable a subject appears to viewers, independent of what they actually say — a low angle can look intimidating, an extreme close-up can look invasive. Choose framing for the subject's dignity, not just visual interest.

Motion and Duration

How long a shot lingers on suffering, and whether it's paired with slow motion or a dramatic score, are editorial choices that shape whether the piece reads as reporting or as engineered emotional impact.

Rights, Credit, and Legal Basics

Confirm you have the right to use any image or footage you didn't shoot yourself, including drone footage, which may also carry local flight restrictions worth checking before you fly. Credit photographers and videographers accurately, including freelancers and wire services — uncredited use isn't just an ethical lapse, it can carry real legal exposure for the outlet.

"The camera doesn't just record the story. It makes an argument about what matters in the frame. Make that argument on purpose."

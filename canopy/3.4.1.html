Field Craft

A well-drafted RTI request gets a substantive answer. A vague one gives an unwilling authority an easy excuse to stall. This module is about the practical drafting skill — for the legal scope, exemptions, and appeal process behind the Act itself, see the cross-link below.

For the RTI Act's full legal scope, exemptions, and appeal ladder, see Pillar 4, Module 2.3 (The Right to Information Act in Practice) — this module focuses specifically on drafting an effective request.

Drafting a Request That's Hard to Reject

Vague

"All information about the factory's environmental compliance" — broad enough that an authority can claim it doesn't know what specifically to search for, or take months to compile.

Specific

"Copies of all Environmental Clearance Certificate applications and renewal decisions for [named facility] from [date] to [date]" — names the exact document type, the exact entity, and a bounded date range.

Name the Document Type

Ask for a specific record — an inspection report, a permit file, a meeting minute — not a general subject area.

Bound the Date Range

An unbounded request is easier to deprioritize. A specific window signals you know exactly what you're looking for.

Common Rejection Patterns

The most common rejection isn't a formal exemption claim — it's simply going quiet. A specific, well-bounded request makes silence more obviously indefensible if you escalate to appeal, because there's no ambiguity about what was actually asked for.

"A precise request doesn't just get better answers. It gets you a stronger position if you have to appeal a non-answer."

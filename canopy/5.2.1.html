Field Craft

A defamation threat doesn't have to succeed in court to do damage — the cost and stress of a legal proceeding is often the actual point. Watchdog groups including the Committee to Protect Journalists have documented a rising pattern of what are sometimes called SLAPP suits (strategic lawsuits against public participation) used to burden reporters and outlets with costly proceedings rather than to win a genuine legal argument.

How Defamation Claims Get Used as Pressure

A company or official facing an accountability story doesn't need to win a defamation case to make it expensive and stressful for you to have written it. The strategy often isn't about the merits — it's about the burden of the process itself. Understanding this changes how you prepare: your goal isn't just to be right, it's to be able to demonstrate, quickly and clearly, exactly why you were right.

Pre-Publication Legal Review

Review Before, Not After

Getting a legal read on a genuinely risky accountability piece before publication is far cheaper and calmer than responding to a legal threat afterward.

Separate Fact From Characterization

A factual claim needs a source or document behind it. A characterization ("appears to," "critics say") needs to be clearly framed as such, not blurred into a factual assertion.

Documentation Standards

Every factual claim in an accountability piece should trace back to something you can produce if challenged: a document, a recording, a named source's on-record statement. Retain these systematically, not just in memory — a defamation defense often comes down to how quickly and completely you can show your work.

"You don't need to win the argument before you publish. You need to be able to prove, on demand, exactly how you got there."

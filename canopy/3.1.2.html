Field Craft

A dataset with a clean interface and a professional-looking chart isn't automatically reliable — it just means someone with design skills packaged it. The actual question is whether you know who collected it, how, and whether it holds up against an independent check.

Provenance and Methodology

Who Collected It

A named institution with a documented method is a different kind of source than an unattributed number circulating in a report or presentation.

How It Was Collected

Sensor readings, surveys, and modeled estimates all carry different kinds and degrees of uncertainty — knowing which one you're looking at changes how confidently you can report it.

Known Gaps in Government Data

Under-Reporting Is a Real Risk, Not Just a Cliché

This isn't hypothetical — Module 4.3.1 in this toolkit documents a concrete example: one of the most useful open Bangladeshi weather datasets covers only 1948 to 2014, meaning anything more recent requires a separate, slower formal request. A dataset can be genuinely official and still have real coverage gaps that aren't obvious until you go looking for the most recent year.

Cross-Checking Before You Publish

Before a specific number from a dataset becomes a specific claim in your story, find a second, independent source that roughly agrees with it — a different agency's figure, an academic study, or even a knowledgeable source's estimate. Agreement doesn't prove either source is right, but disagreement is exactly the kind of thing worth chasing down before you publish, not after a correction.

"A dataset earns your trust the same way a human source does: through a track record, a clear method, and agreement with someone else who checked independently."

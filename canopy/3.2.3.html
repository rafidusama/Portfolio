Advanced

Forest loss is one of the easiest environmental changes to prove and one of the hardest to explain — the satellite tells you a patch of forest disappeared, but only ground reporting tells you whether that was a storm, a farmer, or a permit nobody should have issued.

Reading Forest-Loss Alerts

Global Forest Watch's GLAD alerts flag likely forest loss from satellite imagery within days of it happening, giving you a near-real-time lead rather than waiting for an annual report. An alert marks that something changed — it doesn't yet tell you why, which is where your reporting starts.

Natural Loss vs. Illegal Activity

Natural Loss

Storm damage, natural fire, or seasonal dieback tends to show irregular, scattered patterns and often clusters after a known weather event.

Illegal Logging or Encroachment

Often shows more geometric, systematic clearing patterns — straight edges, expanding plots — and tends to occur without a corresponding weather event to explain it.

Neither pattern alone is proof — confirm with a site visit, a local source, or land-ownership records before characterizing an alert as illegal activity in print.

Combining Forest Data With Land Records

An alert showing forest loss inside a designated protected area or Ecologically Critical Area is a much stronger story than loss on ambiguously owned land. Cross-referencing the alert's location against Forest Department protected-area boundaries and any available land-ownership records turns "something changed here" into "something changed here, and it shouldn't have."

"The satellite gives you the where and when. Land records and a site visit give you the why and who."

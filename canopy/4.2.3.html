Field Craft

Bangladesh's Right to Information Act, 2009 gives every citizen a real, legally grounded path to government-held data — this module covers where that right actually applies, what's exempted, and the specific ladder of appeals when a request goes nowhere. Module 3.4.1 in Pillar 3 covers the practical skill of filing; this one covers the legal terrain underneath it.

Scope: Who Must Respond

Government & Statutory Bodies

Ministries, divisions, offices, and any body established by or under an Act — the core, unambiguous scope of the law.

Government or Foreign-Funded Private Organizations

Private organizations run on government funding or foreign funding also fall under the Act, which extends its reach well beyond ministries alone.

Public-Function Contractors

Organizations performing public functions under a government contract are covered — relevant for privatized or outsourced service delivery.

Purely Private Companies

Generally excluded directly, but records a government regulator holds about a private company (audits, filings) may still be requestable from the regulator itself.

What's Exempted (Section 7)

The Eight Excluded Agencies

The Act names eight specific security and intelligence bodies as entirely outside its scope: the National Security Intelligence (NSI), Directorate General of Forces Intelligence (DGFI), Defence Intelligence Units, the Criminal Investigation Department (CID), the Special Security Force (SSF), the intelligence cell of the National Board of Revenue, the Special Branch of Police, and the intelligence cell of the Rapid Action Battalion (RAB) — unless the information sought concerns corruption or human rights violations within these bodies, which remains requestable even then.

Separately, internal "note sheets" used in government decision-making are excluded from the Act's very definition of "information" — they're not exempted so much as outside the law's reach entirely. Any refusal otherwise requires the authority to first get approval from the Information Commission and must allow partial disclosure of any non-exempt portions of a request.

The Global Right to Information Rating has assessed Bangladesh's law as "reasonably good," while specifically flagging these blanket security exemptions as a real weakness in the framework — worth knowing when a refusal invokes one of these categories.

The Appeal Ladder

Step 1 — Request
Filed with the Designated Officer using the prescribed form.
Response Window
20 working days (up to 30 in exceptional cases); if refusing, the officer must explain within 10 working days.
Step 2 — First Appeal
To a senior officer within the same authority, within 30 days, using form "Gaa" (Section 24). Must be resolved within 15 days.
Step 3 — Complaint to IC
To the Information Commission if the appeal fails (Section 25) — can penalize officers and order compensation.
Last Resort
Courts are otherwise barred from a direct RTI denial claim, but an Information Commission decision can be challenged via writ petition to the Supreme Court (Section 29).

Realistic Expectations

Most requests are free, though the government may set reasonable fees in consultation with the Information Commission — when a fee applies, it's typically payable through a designated bank channel within five working days of notification. Build the fee-payment window into your own timeline expectations, not just the response window.

"The law gives you a specific ladder to climb when a request is refused. Most journalists stop at the first rung. Knowing there are three more is often the actual advantage."

Field Craft

Climate displacement in Bangladesh is rarely a single dramatic departure. It's usually a sequence: a household adapts, adapts again, and eventually moves — often more than once, often incompletely, often without ever being formally counted as "displaced" anywhere. Reporting it as a systemic pattern rather than a one-time snapshot is the core skill here.

Salinity Intrusion Mechanics

Rising soil and water salinity in coastal areas is driven by a mix of sea level rise, upstream freshwater diversion, and commercial shrimp farming practices that deliberately let in saline water. The livelihood impact compounds over seasons: a rice variety fails, then a second, then drinking water sources turn brackish — the decision to leave usually comes after several failed adaptations, not as a first response.

Char Land Formation and Erosion Cycles

Char lands — river-formed sandbars and islands — erode and re-form in cycles that can displace and resettle the same families repeatedly within a single lifetime. Land ownership on a char is often informal or contested, which means a family can lose their home to erosion and have no clear legal claim to the same land when it re-forms years later.

Internal Migration Routes Into Dhaka

Push Factors

Salinity, erosion, and repeated crop failure are the most common drivers cited by displaced households moving toward Dhaka and other major cities.

Landing Points

Informal settlements in Dhaka absorb much of this migration, often themselves in flood-prone or otherwise precarious parts of the city.

Avoiding "Climate Refugee" Framing

"Climate refugee" has no formal legal status under international law, and using it can overstate certainty about a single cause when displacement is usually driven by an overlapping mix of economic, social, and environmental pressures. Naming the specific pressures a household describes is more accurate and more useful to the reader than reaching for the label.

"Ask what they tried before they left. The leaving is rarely the whole story — the adapting usually is."

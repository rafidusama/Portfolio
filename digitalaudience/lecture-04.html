Carpentier · McQuail · Ang

Aggregate or community?

Most media firms see an audience as an aggregate: a collection of individuals who happen to consume the same text at the same time, with no identifying relation between them.

  • The aggregate view. Ang's "taxonomic collective": individuals grouped only because each has one characteristic in common. They do not know each other. They are a statistical population, not a social group.
  • The community view. Members share values, interpretive strategies, and practices. They interact. They have continuity, history, and a sense of identity that persists beyond any single text.
  • The difference matters for platforms. Platforms that cultivate community get higher retention, more UGC, and more durable attention.
  • Both can be true at once. A Prothom Alo article is consumed by an aggregate of anonymous readers and simultaneously by several overlapping communities each reading it through different eyes.
The spectrum of audience collectivity

Three levels of togetherness

01
Social audience
A group sharing media in the same physical or digital space at the same moment: a cinema queue, a WhatsApp group watching a match. Some loose social relation exists, but no sustained identity.
Reveal
02
Community audience
A group sharing a common frame of interpretation, identity, and values organised around a text or genre. They identify as a group, sustain that identity over time, and defend it against outsiders.
Reveal
03
Organised audience
A formal, structured group: a fan club with officers, rules, and membership; an esports guild. Here the audience has become an institution in its own right.
Reveal

In games: a pick-up lobby (social) vs a ranked guild (community) vs an esports organisation with contracts (organised).

Anatomy of a fandom

Core fans (innermost)

FIAWOL: Fandom is a Way of Life. Wiki editors, server admins, fic writers, fan-cam producers. A small number sustain the entire infrastructure everyone else consumes.

Engaged fans

Regular participants: active in forums and Discord, attend events, produce occasional content. Identity is clearly shaped by the fandom but it is one of several identities.

Casual fans

Consume the content, enjoy it, may share it. May self-identify as fans but do not organise their social life around it. The largest active group.

Lurkers (outermost)

The audience for fan content itself. Watch fan-cams, read wikis, browse subreddits. Often invisible in participation counts but numerically dominant.

What fans actually do

01
Textual poaching
Jenkins: fans take characters, worlds, and plots and rewrite them for their own purposes. They are not pirates but readers who refuse to stay passive.
Reveal
02
Paratext production
Fan fiction, fan-cams, tributes, memes, wikis, subtitles, reviews: a second economy of content produced for and by the community, without pay.
Reveal
03
Community maintenance
Someone has to run the server, moderate the forum, archive the fic. Core fans provide invisible infrastructure that casual fans and lurkers depend on entirely.
Reveal
04
Identity performance
Being a fan is a public identity claim. What you ship, which characters you defend, which canon you accept: these signal who you are to other fans.
Reveal

The industry benefits enormously from fan creativity and promotion. Whether this is empowerment or free labour for commercial gain is genuinely contested.

Parasocial and social
Fans form real relationships with each other through the fan object. The imagined community becomes materially real: shared spaces, friendships, events.

McQuail · Gans · taste & distinction

Taste cultures and subcultures

  • Taste culture (Gans). A group of individuals sharing a preference for certain media content. Genre fandoms are taste cultures: horror fans, classical music listeners, literary fiction readers.
  • Subculture. A more oppositional formation: defined partly by its difference from, or resistance to, the dominant culture. Music subcultures built distinct visual styles, values, and media alongside their sounds.
  • Digital subcultures. Online, subcultures form without geographic limits. A Bangladeshi fan of Japanese visual kei music participates in a global subcultural community sustained entirely through platforms.
  • Subcultural capital. Within a subculture, members compete for status through knowledge, rarity, and authenticity, not just consumption. Knowing the B-sides matters more than owning the album.

In music: Bangladesh's underground metal scene is a local subculture with its own aesthetic codes, distrust of mainstream radio, and strong sense of insider identity.

Community in the interactive sector

Game communities: playing together, becoming together

  • The guild as community. An esports guild or MMO clan is a fully organised audience: officers, ranks, shared history, collective identity. Members spend more time in the community than consuming the game alone.
  • Modding as fan labour. Game modders extend, transform, and sometimes surpass the original text. They are the most extreme form of textual poaching: rewriting the source code, not just the narrative.
  • Spectator communities. The 2021 League of Legends World Championship reached 73.8 million peak concurrent viewers. These are not players but fans: a distinct audience community formed around watching others play.
  • Toxicity as community failure. When community norms break down, the social structure of a game audience can produce harassment, gatekeeping, and exclusion — a reminder that community is not automatically positive.

In Bangladesh: PUBG Mobile and Free Fire communities on Facebook and Discord show all three layers: social play groups, organised guilds, and spectator fan channels.

The creator economy sector

The creator's audience is the creator's community

For a creator, the audience is not an anonymous aggregate. It is a community whose loyalty, identity investment, and participation are the creator's primary asset.

01
The parasocial bond at scale
Fans feel they know the creator personally. This parasocial relationship is the basis of trust, and trust is what makes sponsorship deals work. Destroy trust, lose the audience.
Reveal
02
Community as product
A creator's Discord, live-stream chat, and comment section are where the community's identity is performed. The creator produces the space; the audience produces the community.
Reveal
03
The platform dependency trap
The community lives on platforms the creator does not own. If the algorithm changes or the account is suspended, the creator's access to the community is severed. The community belongs to the platform, not the creator.
Reveal
04
Fan labour for creators
Super-chats, memberships, Patreon pledges, merch purchases: the community does economic work for the creator. Whether this is enthusiastic mutual support or a new form of the audience commodity is an open question.
Reveal

Uses and gratifications

People do not just passively receive media. They actively choose it to fulfil specific needs. The key question shifts: not "what does media do to audiences?" but what do audiences do with media?

  • Audiences use media with purpose. They have needs and choose media they expect to satisfy those needs.
  • Media must satisfy social or psychological needs. Information, entertainment, escape, social connection, and identity are the classic gratification categories.
  • Audiences are rational actors. They select among competing media options based on expected gratification.
  • Media compete with each other. A specific medium or genre can only fulfil some needs; audiences move between options.
  • Gratifications sustain habits. If media stops delivering the gratification, the behaviour stops.

In Bangladesh: Bongo satisfies nostalgia and language-specific entertainment needs that global platforms cannot. That is a gratification advantage no algorithm can easily copy.

Parasocial relationships

Audiences form one-sided relationships with media figures. The presenter, the streamer, the creator speaks to you directly. You feel you know them. They have no idea you exist.

  • It feels real. Brain response to a parasocial companion activates similar regions to real-world social bonds. The emotional stakes are genuine even though the relationship is one-directional.
  • Presence intensifies it. Reality TV and live streaming exploit "presence": the illusion that you are watching a non-mediated, real encounter.
  • Trust transfers. Parasocial trust is why influencer marketing works. You trust the recommendation of someone you feel you know, even if you have never met them.
  • Loss is genuinely painful. When a parasocial figure dies, retires, or betrays their audience's values, the grief and anger are proportional to the depth of the relationship built.

A Bangladeshi YouTube creator with 500,000 subscribers has hundreds of thousands of people who feel they know them personally. That is the asset being monetised.

Habit and variable reward

The habit loop and variable reward

01
The cue
A notification, a time of day, a moment of boredom. Platforms design cues that fire at psychologically vulnerable moments: the first thing you reach for in the morning, the gap before sleep.
Reveal
02
The routine
The behaviour itself: opening the app, scrolling the feed, watching the next episode. The smoother and lower-friction the routine, the more likely the habit forms.
Reveal
03
The reward
The payoff: a funny video, a friend's post, a like on your own content. Crucially, the reward is variable. Skinner showed that unpredictable rewards build stronger habits than fixed ones.
Reveal
04
Variable reward in practice
You do not know if the next scroll will be dull or delightful. That uncertainty is the hook. Slot machines use the same mechanism. Natasha Schull called this the "machine zone."
Reveal
05
Boredom as driver
Paasonen shows boredom is not the opposite of engagement: it is often the engine. The low-engagement scroll looking for something interesting is itself a habit that platforms have colonised.
Reveal
06
Habit vs dark pattern
A habit the user consciously chose and can exit is not inherently a dark pattern. A habit engineered to be difficult to break, invisible to the user, or addictive by design crosses the line.

Two more drivers

FOMO and social proof

01
Fear of missing out (FOMO)
FOMO is anxiety about being excluded from events, conversations, and experiences others are having. Social platforms are engineered around it: the unread notification count, the story that expires in 24 hours, the trending topic that everyone else seems to know about.
Reveal
02
Social proof
Humans use others' behaviour as a signal of correct action. Visible like counts, share numbers, trending labels, and "X others are watching this" cues all trigger social proof. Platforms amplify these signals to drive the behaviour they want to measure.
Reveal
03
How they combine
FOMO and social proof reinforce each other: the trending topic triggers FOMO, the view count provides social proof, the combination makes engagement feel obligatory rather than chosen.
Reveal
04
The gratification overlap
Uses and gratifications theory frames these as genuine needs: social integration, surveillance, and identity affirmation. Platforms did not invent these needs. They learned to activate them more efficiently and at greater scale than any prior medium.
Reveal
Hotspot
Hotspot

Spot the dark pattern

Click each feature of this mock app screen. Is it ethical engagement design or a dark pattern?

Autoplay countdown

The next episode starts in 5 seconds unless you actively cancel. Requires deliberate effort to stop. Designed to continue viewing beyond conscious intent.

Personalised recommendations

Showing content matched to your past viewing. Based on declared or inferred preferences. Raises relevance for genuinely interested users.

Hidden cancel button

The unsubscribe option is buried inside three nested menus, uses small grey text, and requires re-confirmation. Makes stopping harder than starting.

Unread notification badge

A red badge showing "99+" alerts that are mostly algorithmic content pushes, not personal messages. Triggers FOMO and compulsive checking.

Content warnings

Upfront labels on content that may be disturbing or inappropriate. Gives the user genuine choice about what to engage with.

Infinite scroll

No natural stopping point in the feed. No page end, no "you're up to date" message. Removes the environmental cue that would naturally end a session.

Drawing the line

Ethical engagement vs exploitation

  • Ethical design. Uses psychological knowledge to help users achieve their own purposes: find content they genuinely enjoy, build skills, stay informed, connect with people they care about.
  • Dark patterns. Use psychological knowledge to keep users in a platform or behaviour longer than they would consciously choose, to extract more data, more attention, or more spending.
  • The test. Would the user endorse this design if they understood it fully? If the answer is no, that is the dividing line.
  • The structural problem. Engagement metrics are what platforms optimise. Maximising engagement maximises revenue. Dark patterns are a feature of the business model, not a bug in the code.

Mobile games in Bangladesh: Candy Crush-style games use variable rewards ethically for casual entertainment. Pay-to-win mechanics targeting spending propensity data to extract real money cross the line.

All of the above. Plus: the invisible feed algorithm as the slot machine lever. Every scroll is a pull with an unpredictable payoff.

A mobile game gives players a reward every time they log in, but the reward is randomly either a common item or a rare one. Which psychological mechanism does this exploit?

B. Variable reward schedules, identified by Skinner and applied directly by game designers, produce more persistent behaviour than fixed rewards precisely because the uncertainty keeps the loop active.
